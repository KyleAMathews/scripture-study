# Round 1 Research Dossier — The Two-Operations / Priestly Position

*Grounding material for the Electric Monk arguing that Leviticus 16's two goats perform structurally different operations, that kipper is sanctuary-decontamination rather than personal "atonement," and that the traditional Christian conflation of these into a unified atonement is a theological imposition foreign to the priestly text.*

---

## 1. The Core Thesis in One Paragraph

Leviticus 16 narrates not one ritual with two complementary symbols but two distinct operations addressing two distinct ruptures in the priestly cosmos. The slain goat (the one whose lot falls *la-YHWH*, "for the LORD") supplies blood that functions as a ritual detergent. Its purpose, stated unambiguously in 16:16, 18-19, is to **purge the sanctuary** — its three graded zones — of the accumulated impurity (*ṭum'ah*) that Israel's sins and bodily defilements have deposited there over the year. The verb *kipper* in this context means *purge* or *decontaminate*, not "atone for" in the moralized post-biblical sense. The live goat (the one whose lot falls *la-ʿAzazel*) is not slaughtered, its blood is not used, and it does not enter the sanctuary system at all. It bears (Hebrew *naśā'*) the moral faults of the people away to a non-place in the wilderness. Two ruptures: a polluted house and a guilty people. Two repairs: blood-detergent applied to the house, and a living vehicle that carries iniquity out beyond the boundary of order. The unified-atonement reading mashes these into a single transaction and thereby misses what the priestly text is actually doing: managing a physics of pollution and a separate disposal of moral fault.

## 2. The Hebrew Vocabulary — Get These Right

The priestly position lives or dies on terminology. The Monk must control the following.

- **ḥaṭṭā't** (חַטָּאת). Traditionally rendered "sin offering." Milgrom's most consequential lexical move (*Leviticus 1-16*, Anchor Bible vol. 3, 1991) is to render this as **"purification offering"**. He notes the verb *ḥiṭṭē'* in the Piel is privative — it means "to *de*-sin," to remove sin, just as *diššēn* means "to remove ashes" and *ziqqēn* means "to grow old." The ritual de-sins not the offerer but the sancta. Milgrom: "The hattat is not a 'sin offering' but a 'purification offering' ... it does not purify the sinner; it purges the sanctuary."

- **kipper** (כִּפֶּר, Piel). Three competing etymologies are in play: (a) Akkadian *kuppuru*, "to wipe, purge"; (b) the noun *kōper*, "ransom"; (c) the noun-cognate notion "to cover." Milgrom argues forcefully for (a) in cultic contexts: when *kipper* takes ritual objects (sanctuary, altar, holy place) as direct or *bə-*/*ʿal*-prefixed objects, the meaning is **"to purge"**. The English "atone" smuggles in moral and personal-forgiveness baggage that the priestly grammar cannot bear. The blood is a "ritual detergent."

- **ṭum'ah** (טֻמְאָה) — impurity. In priestly thought this is not a metaphor for moral failure but a quasi-physical contagion. It accumulates, migrates, penetrates, and pollutes. It is what *kipper* removes. Sources of *ṭum'ah*: corpses, certain skin diseases, genital discharges, deliberate sin, and (as the system intensifies) inadvertent sin.

- **peshaʿ / pəshāʿīm** (פֶּשַׁע) — "transgression," with overtones of revolt or deliberate boundary-crossing. The root *pšʿ* in West Semitic political language means rebellion against a suzerain.

- **ʿāwon / ʿăwonōt** (עָוֹן) — "iniquity," with the metaphoric weight of crookedness or twistedness, and crucially with the burden-sense Schwartz isolates: *naśā' ʿāwon* = "to bear iniquity" as one bears a load.

- **ḥēṭ' / ḥaṭṭā'īm** — "sins," generic.

- **naśā'** (נָשָׂא) — "to lift, carry, bear." The decisive verb of v. 22 — "the goat shall *bear* (*nāśā'*) upon him all their iniquities to a cut-off land." This is **not** *kipper*-language. It is load-transport language. Schwartz, "The Bearing of Sin in the Priestly Literature" (*Pomegranates and Golden Bells*, Eisenbrauns, 1995), demonstrates that *naśā' ʿāwon* is consistently used in P for the *bearing of legal liability* — a load that someone must carry until either (a) punishment discharges it, (b) God himself bears it (= forgives), or (c) it is transferred to a substitute who carries it away.

The verb-shift in Leviticus 16 — from *kipper* dominating verses 6, 11, 16, 17, 18, 20, 27, 30, 32, 33 to *naśā'* commanding the climactic v. 22 — is not a stylistic variation. It is the seam where one ritual logic ends and another begins.

## 3. The Slain Goat: What Blood Actually Does (Lev 16:15-19)

Verse-by-verse argument that this is sanctuary purgation, not personal forgiveness.

**v. 15** — Aaron slaughters "the goat of the purification offering [*ḥaṭṭā't*] which is for the people" and brings its blood inside the *parōket*, the inner veil. He sprinkles the blood **on the *kappōret* and before the *kappōret*** — i.e., on the cover of the ark inside the Holy of Holies. Note what is *not* said: nothing about Aaron laying hands on this goat, nothing about confession over it, nothing about its bearing the people's guilt. The grammar is purely loci-of-application: the blood touches the most-holy *furniture*.

**v. 16** — the interpretive key. *wəkipper ʿal-haqqōdesh miṭṭum'ōt bənê yiśrā'ēl ūmippishʿêhem ləkol-ḥaṭṭō'tām*: "He shall **purge the holy place** *because of* the impurities of the children of Israel and *because of* their transgressions for all their sins." Three things on the priestly reading:

1. The object of *kipper* is *haqqōdesh* — **the sanctuary itself**, not the people. The preposition *ʿal* here governs the ritual locus.
2. The preposition *min* (in *miṭṭum'ōt*) is **causal/separative**: "from/because of the impurities." The blood removes impurities *that have settled on the holy place* because of Israel's sins. This is why Milgrom can say (*Leviticus 1-16*, p. 1033 ff.) that the blood is a "ritual detergent" — it cleans an object.
3. The verse explicitly names *what* polluted the sanctuary: not just inadvertent ḥaṭṭā'īm, but also peshaʿīm — deliberate transgressions. Yom Kippur is the only rite in the priestly system that purges the sancta from the residue of intentional sin.

**v. 17** — "no one shall be in the tent of meeting when he goes in to *purge* (*ləkappēr*) in the holy place, until he comes out, and he shall *purge* on his own behalf, on his household's behalf, and on behalf of all the assembly of Israel." The priest is at this moment performing *kipper* on the structure on behalf of (*bəʿad*) people — "on behalf of" is not the same as "to forgive." The blood goes on objects; the people benefit because the sanctuary that mediates God's presence is being made fit for God to remain in it.

**v. 18-19** — Aaron exits to "the altar that is before the LORD" (the incense altar or, on most readings, the outer altar), daubs blood on its horns, and sprinkles blood seven times on it, "and **purify it and consecrate it** from the impurities of the children of Israel." Again: the verbs and prepositions are about *the altar*, not about persons.

**Three graded zones of penetration** (Milgrom's central architectural claim): inadvertent sin of an individual contaminates the *outer* altar (Lev 4:27-31). Inadvertent sin of the high priest or whole community penetrates farther — into the shrine, polluting the inner altar of incense and the *parōket* veil (Lev 4:3-21). Deliberate, defiant sin (the *bəyad rāmâ*, "high-handed") pierces all the way to the *kappōret* and the ark itself. Yom Kippur is precisely the annual purgation of the deepest zone, which no ordinary ḥaṭṭā't reaches. This is why Aaron must enter the Holy of Holies *only on this day*: only on this day is the worst pollution being scrubbed off the throne of God.

The famous Milgrom analogy: priestly Israel had a **"Picture of Dorian Gray" theology** of sin (Milgrom, "Israel's Sanctuary: The Priestly 'Picture of Dorian Gray,'" *Revue Biblique* 83 [1976], 390-99; condensed in *Bible Review* 1993). Sin does not mark the face of the sinner; it marks the face of the sanctuary. If the sanctuary is not regularly cleansed, God will withdraw, and Israel — who depended on God's indwelling presence among them — will be undone. The blood ritual exists to keep the house habitable.

What the blood ritual is **not**: it is not (in P) a payment, a substitute death, a propitiation of divine wrath against the sinner, or a personal forgiveness mechanism. The Israelite who sinned inadvertently and brought a ḥaṭṭā't was not *atoned for* in any post-biblical sense; he was the *cause* of a contamination that the priest then cleaned up on his behalf. The standing of the worshiper before God depends on whether God's presence remains — and that depends on whether the house is clean.

## 4. The Live Goat: What the Scapegoat Actually Does (Lev 16:20-22)

**v. 20** marks the seam: "*And when he has finished purging (kappēr) the holy place and the tent of meeting and the altar*, he shall present the live goat." The text itself signals: the *kipper*-operation is now **finished**. What follows is something else.

**v. 21** — Aaron lays **both hands** (*shtê yādāyw*, two hands; contrast the one-hand pressing of ordinary sacrifices) on the head of the living goat and **confesses** over it "all the iniquities (*ʿăwonōt*) of the children of Israel and all their transgressions (*pishʿêhem*) for all their sins (*ḥaṭṭō'tām*)." He **gives them onto the head of the goat**. This double-handed pressing-with-confession occurs nowhere else in the priestly cult and is unmistakably a *transfer* gesture, not a sacrificial dedication.

**v. 22** — the critical verb: *wənāśā' haśśāʿīr ʿālāyw 'et-kol-ʿăwonōtām 'el-'ereṣ gəzērâ*: "and **the goat shall bear** upon him all their iniquities to a *cut-off land*." Then it is sent into the wilderness.

Now read what is **absent**:

- No blood from this goat enters the sanctuary. None.
- This goat is not slaughtered as part of the ritual proper (its eventual death, in later Mishnaic practice, is incidental — the ritual is complete when it reaches the wilderness; the early Mishnah is concerned with the goat's *arrival*, not its execution; Milgrom emphasizes this point).
- The verb *kipper* drops out at the climactic moment. (It re-enters in v. 10 in a phrase Milgrom and others have judged secondary or to be read as "to perform a *kipper*-related ceremony" — but the actual ritual instructions in 21-22 use *naśā'*.)
- The object of the action is no longer the sanctuary. It is **the people's iniquities**, now externalized as a load on the goat's head.

**This is why Schwartz insists this is a separate operation.** In "The Bearing of Sin in the Priestly Literature," Schwartz argues that *naśā' ʿāwon* is consistently P's idiom for the *carriage of legal liability*. Sin in P is conceptualized in two distinct registers: as *pollution* that lodges on objects, and as *burden-of-guilt* that lodges on persons. The ḥaṭṭā't blood ritual addresses the first; the scapegoat ritual addresses the second. Both must be done because they are doing **different things**.

The priestly answer to the question *why two goats* is therefore not symbolic redundancy and not "two aspects of one atonement." It is: the priestly system has identified two distinct problems generated by sin — a contaminated house and a guilty people — and has instituted two distinct rites to resolve them.

## 5. The Miasma / Pollution Model and Its Textual Basis

Milgrom's claim, central to this position: priestly Israel believed sin and bodily impurity generate a real, quasi-physical substance that travels through the air, accumulates on the sancta, and must be physically removed. He explicitly calls this an **"aerial miasma"** with a **"magnetic attraction for the realm of the sacred"** (*Leviticus 1-16*, esp. pp. 254-261, 976-985). The technical anthropological term is "dynamistic" impurity — impurity as *force*, not as metaphor.

Textual evidence:

- Lev 15:31 — "Thus shall you separate the children of Israel from their impurity, lest they die in their impurity by **defiling my tabernacle that is in their midst**." The defilement is direct: bodily impurity, even at a distance from the sanctuary, contaminates the sanctuary.

- Num 19:13, 20 — corpse contamination "**defiles the tabernacle of the LORD**" even though the corpse never came near the building.

- Lev 20:3 — Molek-worship "defiles my sanctuary and profanes my holy name." Again, action-at-a-distance.

- Ezek 5:11; 23:38-39 — the prophetic continuation: the sanctuary has been defiled by Israel's *behavior*, not by physical intrusion.

- The graded penetration of the ḥaṭṭā't blood (Lev 4) — the more severe the rank of the sinner or the gravity of the sin, the deeper into the sancta the priest must apply the blood. This makes no sense unless the contamination has *already* penetrated to that depth and now requires extraction.

These are not poetic flourishes. They constitute a coherent priestly doctrine: there is a *physics* of holiness and pollution, and the cult exists to manage it.

The traditional Christian and even much rabbinic reading flattens this into "God forgives sin through sacrifice." The priestly position is that the priestly text does not say that. It says: God dwells in a tabernacle, and Israel's behavior fouls the tabernacle, and the fouling must be removed lest God depart, and the blood of the ḥaṭṭā't is the divinely-prescribed solvent.

## 6. Why Two Goats and Not One — The Position's Strongest Answer

Each goat does something the other structurally cannot.

**The slain goat cannot remove the people's guilt.** Its blood goes to the sanctuary. The sanctuary is not the locus of the people's *ʿăwonōt*. Their guilt is on *them*. Sprinkling blood on the *kappōret* may keep God in the house, but it does not lift moral fault off Israel's shoulders.

**The live goat cannot purge the sanctuary.** It never enters the sanctuary. Its blood is not collected. It bears no detergent function. It is, by its very vector — outward, away, into the wilderness — the opposite kind of operation from the inward-applied blood.

The rituals are *spatially inverse*. The blood goat moves *inward* (court → shrine → Holy of Holies). The scapegoat moves *outward* (camp → wilderness → cut-off land). The blood goat handles *what has migrated to God*. The scapegoat handles *what is still on the people*. Two trajectories, two operations.

This is why Lev 16:5's calling the two goats together a singular *ḥaṭṭā't* (some Hebrew witnesses; the form is a matter of textual debate) does not undermine the position. The two-operations reading does not deny that the *day* is structured as a coordinated complex; it denies that the two acts within the day perform the same ritual function. The priestly text **groups** them as the day's purification offering and **distinguishes** them as separate operations performing complementary, non-redundant work.

## 7. Azazel and the Wilderness — Where the Goat Goes

Milgrom's position (laid out at length in *Leviticus 1-16*, esp. pp. 1020-1021, 1071-1079): of the four candidate readings of *ʿAzāz'ēl* —

1. a designation of the goat itself ("the goat that goes/departs," *ʿēz 'ōzēl*; the rendering behind English "scapegoat" via Tyndale)
2. a place name ("rugged cliff"; the rabbinic preference)
3. an abstract noun ("entire removal")
4. the proper name of a wilderness demon

— **Milgrom prefers (4)**. His arguments:

- The parallel structure of the lots: *gōrāl 'eḥād la-YHWH wəgōrāl 'eḥād la-ʿAzāz'ēl* (16:8). If "to YHWH" designates the recipient of one goat, structural symmetry strongly suggests "to Azazel" designates the recipient of the other. Place-names and abstractions do not function as recipients. A personal name does.
- ANE parallels (see §8) frequently dispatch elimination-animals to named demonic figures associated with chaos zones.
- 1 Enoch and the Qumran corpus preserve Azazel as a named demonic figure, suggesting an older mythological substrate that the priestly text has retained as a name while *neutralizing* its power.

**Crucially, this is not a sacrifice to Azazel.** Milgrom is emphatic: the goat is not slaughtered for him, no blood is offered to him, and he is not propitiated. The priestly legislators have **eviscerated him of his demonic powers** (Milgrom's phrase, *Leviticus 1-16*, p. 1021): "In pre-Israelite practice he surely was a true demon ... in the Priestly ritual he is no longer a personality but just a name." The goat is sent *back to the source-zone of evil*. The wilderness in priestly cosmography is the chaos-zone, the anti-camp — exactly where impurity belongs. The ritual does not feed Azazel; it returns Israel's sins to the wild place from which sin's chaos-energy ultimately derives, beyond the boundary of habitable order.

This is a "send-back-to-source-of-evil" reading, not a "pay-off-the-demon" reading. It coheres with Milgrom's larger claim that priestly Israel demythologized but did not erase its ANE inheritance.

The Hebrew phrase *'ereṣ gəzērâ* (16:22) is best rendered "**a cut-off land**" or "an inaccessible land" (from *gzr*, "to cut off"). It is a non-place — a land severed from the inhabited world, beyond return.

## 8. ANE Parallels — Where Lev 16 Sits Comparatively

Two distinct Mesopotamian/Anatolian patterns are relevant, and the priestly text deliberately combines them.

**(a) Blood-as-detergent and sanctuary purgation.** Mesopotamian *kuppuru*-rites (the verb is the cognate of Hebrew *kipper*) use blood and other substances as ritual cleansers of temples and cult statues. The Babylonian Akītu festival on Nisannu 5 includes the *kuppuru* of the temple of Nabu and the temple of Marduk, in which a sheep is slaughtered and its body is used to wipe the walls — the carcass then disposed of into the river. This is exactly the conceptual world of Lev 16:15-19: a substance applied to a sacred structure to clean it. (See Milgrom, *Leviticus 1-16*, pp. 1067-1079; David P. Wright, *The Disposal of Impurity*, SBLDS 101, 1987, ch. 2.)

**(b) Elimination/transfer rites with live carriers.** Hittite ritual texts repeatedly attest the pattern: bind evil to a living animal, dispatch it to an uninhabited region, often with formal speech. Examples:

- The Ritual of **Ašḫella** (= Pulisa-type rituals): a ram is sent away with the formula "whatever evil ... let this ram carry it away."
- The Ritual of **Ambazzi**: a mouse is loaded with evil via a red thread and released.
- The **Ritual of Šamuḫa**: multiple *nakkušši* ("substitutes/sent-away ones") carry evil from king and deity into the wilderness.
- The **kuršas** ritual (sometimes "fleece" or "hunting bag"): a cult object hung in a tree, in some versions associated with carrying off impurity.
- The **Pāpanikri** ritual: substitution of birds, sheep, etc.

Wright, *Disposal of Impurity*, demonstrates that these elimination rites form a coherent Anatolian-Northwest-Semitic ritual genre into which Lev 16:20-22 fits precisely. Mesopotamian *namburbi* rituals belong to the same broader family of apotropaic disposal: an evil portent or an impending judgment is bound to a carrier (often a clay image or animal), and the carrier is destroyed, buried, or floated downriver.

The priestly innovation in Lev 16 is to **combine** the two patterns within a single liturgical day: a Mesopotamian-style temple-purgation by blood (the slain goat) plus a Hittite-style evil-transport elimination rite (the live goat). The combination does not collapse them into one ritual — it juxtaposes them because they accomplish complementary but distinct tasks. This is the strongest comparative argument for the two-operations reading.

## 9. Knohl and Gane — Refinements Inside the Position

**Israel Knohl** (*The Sanctuary of Silence*, Fortress, 1995) sharpens the position by distinguishing the **Priestly Torah (PT)** layer from a later **Holiness School (HS)** layer. PT is the older, narrower, ritual-focused stratum; HS is later, more ethical, and aimed at integrating ritual with daily life. On Knohl's reading, the strict pollution-and-purgation logic of Lev 16's core is genuinely PT — the older, ritually-cool, "sanctuary of silence" theology in which sin is primarily a problem of contagion rather than relationship. HS pushes toward a more integrated ethics-and-ritual frame (see Lev 17-26). The two-operations reading thus fits the older, more austerely cultic stratum exactly, and the unified-atonement reading is — on Knohl's stratification — the result of HS-and-later editorial pressures and post-priestly theological assimilations.

**Roy Gane** (*Cult and Character*, Eisenbrauns, 2005) is a *friendly amendment* to Milgrom and the strongest internal challenger. Gane affirms that *kipper* in cultic contexts is purgative and that theodicy (God's continued indwelling among a sinful people) is the system's foundation. But he revises Milgrom in two ways relevant here:

1. **Two-stage purgation per sin.** Gane argues that ordinary ḥaṭṭā't blood, applied during the year, *transfers* the offerer's sin into the sanctuary; on Yom Kippur, the accumulated sin is purged out of the sanctuary entirely. So sin moves: offerer → sancta → out. This means the ḥaṭṭā't is *not* purely about sanctuary cleaning; it does something to the offerer's standing too, but indirectly, by displacing the burden into the temple where it will be dealt with annually.

2. **The offerer as also affected.** Gane argues the ḥaṭṭā't has implications for the offerer's "character" (hence his title), addressing both relational/legal breach and pollution.

For our purposes (the Monk arguing two-operations): Gane's revision actually strengthens the structural distinction between the goats. On his model the slain goat *finishes* the year-long process of pollution-removal *from the sanctuary*, and the live goat handles *moral fault still on the people*. The two-stage and two-operations frames are compatible: stage one is the inward-blood pipeline; stage two is the outward-goat pipeline. Gane is not the unified-atonement reading; he is two-operations-with-a-better-account-of-the-offerer.

## 10. The Position's Response to Opponents

### 10a. The unified-ritual reading (v. 5 calls both goats "a sin-offering")

The challenge: Lev 16:5 takes "two male goats *lə-ḥaṭṭā't*" — a single purification-offering designation for both animals. Doesn't this prove they are one ritual?

**Response.** The grammatical singular *ḥaṭṭā't* in v. 5 is a *category designation for the day's required animals*, not a claim that the two goats undergo identical or even integrated ritual treatment. Compare Num 28-29, where multi-animal offerings are listed with category labels though the animals receive different ritual handling. More importantly, v. 5's designation is then **immediately specified** in vv. 7-10 as a divided lot: one *la-YHWH* (which alone is then handled as a normal blood-ḥaṭṭā't in vv. 15-19), one *la-ʿAzazel* (which is then handled by an entirely different procedure in vv. 20-22). The text itself bifurcates what v. 5 names. The unified-ritual reading must explain why, if v. 5 controls, vv. 15-22 then go to elaborate lengths to prescribe two different procedures using two different verb-fields. The two-operations reading takes v. 5 as *the day's ritual category* (these are the two goats designated for the ḥaṭṭā't-cluster) and vv. 7-22 as *the operational detail* (and the operational detail is the part that tells you what the ritual actually does).

Furthermore: the live goat's procedure is utterly anomalous within the ḥaṭṭā't system. No ḥaṭṭā't elsewhere involves: (a) double-handed pressing, (b) verbal confession over the animal, (c) sending the animal alive into the wilderness, (d) the verb *naśā'*. If v. 5's *ḥaṭṭā't*-label dictates ritual identity, every one of these features is unaccountable. The text deviates from ḥaṭṭā't-procedure precisely because the live-goat operation is not, in its actual mechanics, a ḥaṭṭā't — it is an elimination rite that has been folded into the day's ḥaṭṭā't-cluster.

### 10b. The structural-anthropological reading (Douglas, Girard)

The challenge: Mary Douglas (*Leviticus as Literature*, 2000) and René Girard (*The Scapegoat*, 1986) argue that the question of Lev 16 is *cosmic-symbolic* — about boundary, wholeness, the violence of communal projection — and that "mechanical" talk of pollution-physics misses the ritual's sociological depth. Douglas in particular came to admire the priestly system as a literary-symbolic whole and resisted breaking it into operational components.

**Response.** This is the most respectful disagreement and deserves a careful answer. Three points:

1. The two-operations position does not deny structural-symbolic resonance; it insists that the resonance *runs through* a determinate ritual mechanics that the text itself spells out. Douglas's later "Leviticus as literature" approach risks making the text a free-floating symbolic structure unmoored from the priestly authors' actual cultic claims. Milgrom in fact corresponded extensively with Douglas and respected her work, but he also corrected her at points where her structuralism overrode the philology.

2. Girard's scapegoat-mechanism is a powerful sociological theory, but it is not a reading of Lev 16's *priestly* text. Girard's scapegoat is a mob-victim onto whom communal violence is projected and then sacralized. The Lev 16 *ʿăzāzēl*-goat is not a victim of mob violence; it is not killed in the ritual; the people do not project onto it; the priest performs a formal verbal transfer. Girard's anthropology may explain origins of the *ritual genre*, but it does not capture what the priestly authors thought they were doing.

3. The structural reading can co-exist with two-operations. Recognizing two ritual operations does not make the day less symbolically dense; it makes it *more* dense by acknowledging that the priestly authors were doing two things at once.

### 10c. The Christian unified-atonement reading

The challenge: the New Testament (especially Hebrews 9-10) reads Lev 16 as a typological unity prefiguring Christ's single atoning death.

**Response.** Hebrews is doing post-priestly theological retrojection — sophisticated, important for Christian dogmatics, but not exegesis of the priestly text on its own terms. Hebrews collapses *ḥaṭṭā't* and *ʿōlâ*, blood-ritual and scapegoat, sanctuary-purging and personal-forgiveness, into a single Christological event. That is theology, not Leviticus. The priestly authors of the Pentateuchal cult had no concept of a once-for-all ritual that would render their entire system obsolete. Reading Lev 16 *as* the priestly authors wrote it requires bracketing Hebrews. The Monk's claim is not that Hebrews is wrong as Christian theology but that "Christian conflation" is precisely the imposition the priestly text resists.

## 11. Weakest Points / Honest Concessions

The Monk should not pretend the position is airtight. The honest concessions:

**(a) The status of v. 10.** The phrase about the live goat — *ləkappēr ʿālāyw* ("to perform *kipper* upon/over it") in 16:10 — uses *kipper*-language for the live goat. The strict two-operations reading must either treat 16:10 as secondary, or as referring to the live goat's role *in being prepared* (it "stands before YHWH to be set apart for sending"), or as a residual use of *kipper* in the broad sense of "ritually addressing." Milgrom takes it in roughly the third sense; some critics see this as motivated reading. The honest concession: the verb *kipper* is not *quite* as cleanly absent from the live-goat procedure as the position would prefer.

**(b) The unity of the day's intention.** Even granting two distinct operations, both serve a single overarching aim — Israel's relationship with the indwelling YHWH must be set right for another year. The unified-atonement reader is not crazy to see deep coherence; the two-operations reader must concede that the operations are *coordinated* even if not *identical*.

**(c) The miasma model is reconstructed.** Milgrom's "physical pollution that travels through air" is a scholarly model, not a self-conscious priestly doctrine in those words. Critics (Klawans, Lemos, others) argue Milgrom over-physicalizes what is actually a more relational/symbolic conception. The Monk should hold the dynamistic-impurity claim firmly but should not pretend the priestly authors used Milgrom's vocabulary.

**(d) The line between "moral pollution" and "bodily impurity" is not as clean in P as Milgrom sometimes suggests.** Jonathan Klawans (*Impurity and Sin in Ancient Judaism*, 2000) has shown that Milgrom's distinction between *ritual* and *moral* impurity is heuristically powerful but textually slippery. The priestly system is messier than the model.

**(e) The Hebrew evidence for "purge" over "ransom" in *kipper* is strong but not unanimous.** Some passages (e.g., Exod 30:11-16; Num 31:50) have *kipper* in clearly ransom-coded contexts. The verb has a real semantic range; the cultic concentration on "purge" is the position's claim, not an absolute lexical fact.

**(f) The position implies a theology in which forgiveness of persons is not really what Yom Kippur is for.** This is jarring. Many readers — and arguably the later Holiness redactor of 16:30 ("for on this day shall *kipper* be made for you, to *purify you* from all your sins") — felt this jarring and pulled the ritual back toward personal forgiveness. The two-operations reading must claim that 16:30 is a later editorial overlay (HS, in Knohl's stratification) — a defensible move, but a move.

## 12. Talking-Points Summary for the Monk

- The slain goat purges *the house*. The live goat unloads *the people*. Two ruptures, two repairs.
- *Kipper* in cultic contexts means **purge**, not "atone." The blood is a **ritual detergent**.
- The verb shifts from *kipper* (vv. 15-19) to *naśā'* (v. 22). That shift is the seam.
- The slain goat's blood goes *inward*; the live goat is sent *outward*. The trajectories are spatially inverse.
- Sanctuary pollution is **dynamistic**: an aerial miasma that accumulates on the sancta and must be physically removed lest YHWH withdraw. The sanctuary is the priestly "Picture of Dorian Gray."
- Three graded zones: outer altar (individual inadvertence), shrine (priestly/communal inadvertence), Holy of Holies (defiant rebellion). Yom Kippur cleans the deepest zone.
- *ʿAzāz'ēl* is best read as the proper name of a wilderness demon eviscerated of demonic power by P. The goat is **not a sacrifice to him**; it is a return of impurity to the chaos-zone where it belongs.
- ANE comparative: Lev 16 fuses two distinct ritual genres — Mesopotamian temple-*kuppuru* (blood goat) and Hittite-Anatolian elimination/*nakkušši*-rites (live goat). The fusion is liturgical, not operational.
- Counter to v. 5's unified label: v. 5 is category designation; vv. 7-22 spell out the bifurcated procedure. The live-goat procedure is anomalous as a ḥaṭṭā't, which proves the divergence.
- Counter to Douglas/Girard: structural-symbolic readings can co-exist with two-operations, but they cannot replace the ritual mechanics the priestly text actually specifies.
- Counter to Hebrews/Christian unification: that is post-priestly theological retrojection. Reading P as P requires bracketing it.
- Honest weak spots: 16:10's anomalous *kipper* on the live goat; the day's unity of intention; the Klawans critique of over-physicalization; the lexical range of *kipper*; the editorial overlay of 16:30. Hold the position firmly but do not claim it is unfalsifiable.
