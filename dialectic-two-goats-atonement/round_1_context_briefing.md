## 1. The Question

> Leviticus 16 prescribes the Day of Atonement (Yom Kippur). The high priest takes **two goats** from the congregation. He casts lots: one falls "to the LORD," the other "to Azazel" (16:8). The first goat is slaughtered and its blood is brought into the Holy of Holies and sprinkled on the *kapporet* (mercy seat); the second is presented alive, has all the iniquities of Israel confessed over its head, and is sent into the wilderness, "bearing upon him all their iniquities to a land cut off" (16:22).
>
> **Why two goats? What is the rite actually doing?**

The text itself names both goats together as a single *ḥaṭṭā't* in 16:5. But the procedures applied to each are radically different — one is killed at the altar with blood applied inward to the sanctuary; the other is kept alive with sins confessed and is driven outward into the wilderness. The vocabulary shifts at the seam: *kipper* dominates the blood ritual, *naśā'* ("bear, carry") commands the scapegoat ritual.

This is the live tension the dialectic addresses.

## 2. The Three Positions (Brief)

The dialectic runs three monks because the position-space here is genuinely tri-axial, not binary. Each monk has its own full dossier; this is orientation only.

**Monk A — The Two-Operations / Priestly Position (Milgrom-stream).** The two goats perform structurally different operations on different objects. The blood goat *purges the sanctuary* of impurity that has accumulated there ("kipper" as ritual detergent). The live goat *bears the people's iniquities* away to the wilderness. Two distinct ruptures (polluted house, guilty people), two distinct repairs. The traditional "one atonement" reading is theological retrojection that flattens the priestly text's own precise distinctions. Key thinkers: Jacob Milgrom, Roy Gane, Baruch Schwartz, Israel Knohl.

**Monk B — The Unified-Ritual / Canonical Position.** The text itself calls both goats together a singular *ḥaṭṭā't* (16:5). They are one rite in two visible movements, addressing sin in its full structural reality (defilement + guilt + bearing) through one integrated act displayed in two complementary aspects. The Mishnah, Hebrews, the rabbinic and Christian commentary traditions, and modern canonical readers all read it this way. Functional differentiation within a unified rite is not separation into rites. Key thinkers: Brevard Childs, Jay Sklar, Nobuyoshi Kiuchi, Jon Levenson, Gordon Wenham, the author of Hebrews.

**Monk C — The Anthropological / Structural Position.** The "two operations vs. one act" debate is a category error. Both theological readings assume the rite is doing *theological mechanics* (whether singular or dual). The rite is in fact doing **structural-anthropological work**: cosmic boundary maintenance (Douglas), communal violence management (Girard), production of a ritualized people (Bell), spatial systematics (J. Z. Smith). The two goats trace a single bidirectional operation — one centripetal (blood inward to the cosmic center), one centrifugal (body outward to the chaos-zone) — that re-articulates the difference between inside and outside. Theological language is the priestly community's secondary self-description of work that is more fundamentally anthropological. Key thinkers: Mary Douglas, René Girard, Catherine Bell, Walter Burkert, Hubert/Mauss, Jonathan Z. Smith.

## 3. The Shared Textual Data (What All Three Positions Must Engage)

These facts are stipulated. Any monk that ignores or denies them is failing to inhabit the actual debate.

### 3.1 The Procedural Sequence (Lev 16:5-22)

1. **v. 5** — Aaron takes "two goats *for a sin offering*" (the noun is grammatically singular: *lə-ḥaṭṭā't*, not *lə-ḥaṭṭā'ōt*). One ram for a burnt offering.
2. **v. 7** — Both goats are *presented together* "before the LORD at the door of the tent of meeting."
3. **v. 8** — Lots are cast: one *la-YHWH* ("for the LORD"), one *la-ʿAzāz'ēl* ("for Azazel"). Whatever Azazel is — demon, place, abstract noun — the lots are syntactically parallel.
4. **v. 9-10** — The LORD's-lot goat is offered as a sin offering. The Azazel-lot goat "shall be presented alive before the LORD, *to make atonement upon it* (לְכַפֵּר עָלָיו), to send it away into the wilderness to Azazel." (Note: *kipper*-language *does* appear here over the live goat — a verse the two-operations position must handle carefully.)
5. **v. 11-14** — Aaron slaughters his bull (his own purification offering); brings incense behind the veil; sprinkles bull's blood on the *kapporet*.
6. **v. 15** — Aaron slaughters the LORD's goat; brings its blood inside the veil; sprinkles it on the *kapporet* and before the *kapporet*.
7. **v. 16** — *"And he shall make atonement (kipper) for the holy place because of the impurities (ṭum'ōt) of the children of Israel and because of their transgressions (peshaʿīm) for all their sins (ḥaṭṭā'ōt)."* The object of *kipper* is grammatically the sanctuary; the *cause* is Israel's sins/impurities.
8. **v. 18-19** — Aaron daubs blood on the horns of the altar and sprinkles seven times to "cleanse it and consecrate it" from the impurities.
9. **v. 20** — *"And when he has made an end of atoning (kallotō mikkappēr) for the holy place, and the tent of meeting, and the altar, he shall present the live goat."* This verse chains the two phases: the live goat ritual begins after the kipper of the sanctuary is "ended."
10. **v. 21** — Aaron lays *both hands* (*shtê yādāyw*; unique double-handed pressing in the priestly cult) on the live goat's head, **confesses** "all the iniquities (*ʿăwonōt*) of the children of Israel and all their transgressions (*pishʿêhem*) for all their sins (*ḥaṭṭō'tām*)," puts them on the goat's head, and sends it away by the hand of a "ready man."
11. **v. 22** — *"And the goat shall bear (nāśā') upon him all their iniquities to a cut-off land (ʾereṣ gəzērâ); and he shall let go the goat in the wilderness."*
12. **v. 27** — The bodies of the slain bull and goat are carried *outside the camp* and burned.
13. **v. 30** — *"For on this day shall atonement (kipper) be made for you, to cleanse you (ləṭahēr); from all your sins before the LORD shall ye be clean."* (Holiness-school overlay, on Knohl's stratification.)
14. **v. 34** — "An everlasting statute... once a year."

### 3.2 Hebrew Vocabulary the Monks Must Control

- **ḥaṭṭā't** (חַטָּאת) — translated "sin offering" or (Milgrom) "purification offering." The verb *ḥiṭṭē'* in Piel is privative ("to de-sin").
- **kipper** (כִּפֶּר, Piel) — translated "make atonement" / "purge" / "cover" / "ransom." Three competing etymologies (Akkadian *kuppuru* "wipe"; noun *kōper* "ransom"; the "cover" sense). The verb is the central terminus technicus of the rite. **Sklar argues the verb is integrated (purge AND ransom in one act); Milgrom argues it is purgative in cultic contexts.**
- **ṭum'ah** (טֻמְאָה) — impurity. Whether quasi-physical contagion (Milgrom's "dynamistic" reading) or relational/symbolic (Klawans's critique) is contested.
- **peshaʿ** (פֶּשַׁע) — transgression, with overtones of rebellion/revolt.
- **ʿāwon** (עָוֹן) — iniquity, with the metaphoric weight of crookedness AND of a burden to be carried (*naśā' ʿāwon*).
- **naśā'** (נָשָׂא) — to lift, carry, bear. The decisive verb of v. 22. Schwartz argues it is consistently P's idiom for the *carriage of legal liability*. Kiuchi argues it is a *constituent* of *kipper*, not a separate concept (citing Lev 10:17 which fuses the two).
- **kapporet** (כַּפֹּרֶת) — the lid of the ark, traditionally "mercy seat." Etymologically related to *kipper*; whether the connection is "place of covering," "place of purgation," or "place of ransom" depends on the reading of *kipper*.
- **ʾereṣ gəzērâ** (אֶרֶץ גְּזֵרָה, v. 22) — "cut-off land" (from *gzr*, "to cut off"). A non-place beyond the boundary of inhabited order.
- **ʿAzāz'ēl** (עֲזָאזֵל, v. 8, 10, 26) — four candidate readings: (1) "the goat that goes" (basis of "scapegoat" via Tyndale); (2) a place name ("rugged cliff"); (3) abstract noun ("entire removal"); (4) proper name of a wilderness demon (Milgrom's preference; supported by 1 Enoch and Qumran). The lots' parallel structure (*la-YHWH / la-ʿAzāz'ēl*) suggests recipient-symmetry.

### 3.3 The Spatial Choreography

The rite traces a precise topology through three zones:
- **Holy of Holies** (innermost; God's throne; entered only on this day)
- **Holy Place / outer altar / camp** (the ordered human zone)
- **Wilderness** (chaos / un-cultivated / beyond-the-boundary)

The slain goat's *blood* moves inward (camp → Holy Place → Holy of Holies). The live goat's *body* moves outward (camp → wilderness → cut-off land). This bidirectionality is structural and uncontested across all three positions; what is contested is *what it means*.

### 3.4 The Anomalies Each Position Must Handle

- **The singular *lə-ḥaṭṭā't* in v. 5.** Unified reading takes this as decisive; two-operations reads it as category-designation that the procedural detail then bifurcates.
- **The *kipper*-language over the live goat in v. 10.** Unified reading takes this as confirmation that both phases are *kipper*; two-operations reads it as residual or referring to preparation/standing-before-YHWH.
- **The verb-shift to *naśā'* in v. 22.** Two-operations reads this as the seam between operations; unified reading reads it as a constituent aspect of *kipper* (per Lev 10:17).
- **The double-handed pressing and verbal confession over the live goat (v. 21).** Unique in the priestly cult. Two-operations reads this as transfer-gesture distinctive to elimination rites; unified reading reads it as the climax of one ritual that addresses sin's threefold structure (defilement + guilt + bearing); structural reading reads it as the Girardian projection-act.
- **The chaining language of v. 20** ("when he has made an end of atoning... he shall present the live goat"). Unified reading takes this as continuity-marker; two-operations takes "end of atoning" as exactly the seam.
- **The Holiness-school overlay in v. 30.** Knohl and Milgrom read this as later editorial pressure pulling the rite toward personal forgiveness; unified readers read it as the rite's own self-interpretation.

### 3.5 The Ancient Near Eastern Comparative Field

The rite sits within a documented genre.
- **Mesopotamian *kuppuru* rites** (cognate with Hebrew *kipper*): blood and other substances applied to temples/cult statues to clean them. Babylonian Akītu festival: a sheep is slaughtered and used to wipe the temple walls; carcass disposed in the river.
- **Hittite elimination rites** (Ašḫella, Ambazzi, Šamuḫa, kuršas): evil bound to a living animal which is dispatched to uninhabited regions, often with formal speech.
- **Mesopotamian *namburbi*** rituals: apotropaic disposal of evil portents.

The two-operations position reads Lev 16 as deliberately *combining* two distinct ANE genres (temple-purgation + elimination rite) into one liturgical day — the combination is liturgical, not operational. The structural position reads the comparative parallels as evidence that Lev 16 instantiates a pan-ANE family of boundary-management rituals, with Israelite distinctives layered on top.

## 5. The Deepest Framing of the Contradiction

The surface question is "two goats or one rite?" The orchestrator judges that the *deepest* version of the contradiction is:

> **What kind of thing IS Leviticus 16, such that the question "what does the rite do?" can be answered at all?**

Each monk inhabits a different answer to this prior question:
- **Monk A** (priestly): a coded ritual technology with a determinate cultic mechanics, whose precise vocabulary and procedure were known to the priestly authors; the right reading is the one most faithful to that cultic mechanics.
- **Monk B** (canonical): an integrated theological-liturgical witness whose meaning is its canonical wholeness; the right reading is the one that grasps the rite as the priestly text and its faithful inheritors received it — as one act of atonement displayed in two visible movements.
- **Monk C** (structural): a ritualized cultural-cosmic operation whose theological self-description is secondary to its structural-anthropological function; the right reading is the one that names what the rite *does* to bodies, boundaries, and communities, not what its priestly authors *said* it does.

This is the axis. The monks should argue not just for their answer to "what does the rite do?" but for their answer to "what kind of thing makes that question askable in the first place?" Round 1 will probably surface that the deepest tension is meta-hermeneutical: it is about *what counts as understanding a ritual text*.

## 6. Framing Corrections — Degenerate Framings to Avoid

Each monk will receive its own specific framing corrections in Phase 2. But all three should avoid:

- **The "literal vs. symbolic" debate.** All three positions agree the rite is doing *real* work; they disagree about what register that work operates in.
- **The "Jewish vs. Christian" debate.** The canonical-unified position is grounded in pre-Christian Jewish material (v. 5, Mishnah Yoma) before it engages Hebrews. The structural position is post-confessional and treats Jewish and Christian theological readings symmetrically.
- **The "ancient vs. modern" debate.** All three positions are modern scholarly reconstructions; the question is which best illuminates the priestly text.
- **The "kipper means X" lexical debate as the whole question.** Lexical disputes are downstream of the prior interpretive frame; arguing about *kipper* in isolation misses what the positions are actually disagreeing about.
