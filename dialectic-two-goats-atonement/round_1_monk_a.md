# The Two Operations of Leviticus 16

## a. What Leviticus 16 Is

Leviticus 16 is a priestly cultic instruction text. It prescribes, for a single liturgical day, two structurally distinct ritual operations that address two structurally distinct ruptures in the priestly cosmos: a polluted sanctuary and an iniquity-laden people. The right reading of this text is the one most faithful to the priestly authors' own carefully maintained terminological and procedural distinctions — the distinctions encoded in *ḥaṭṭā't* versus *ʿōlâ*, *kipper* versus *naśā'*, *ṭum'ah* versus *ʿāwon*, inward-applied blood versus outward-driven body. These distinctions are not stylistic. They are load-bearing. The priestly authors were technical specialists operating a system of remarkable internal coherence, and the determinative interpretive question is not "what does Christian or rabbinic theology say the rite means?" nor "what does anthropological structuralism say it does beneath what its authors thought?" — both are *meta*-frames the priestly authors did not endorse — but rather: **what do the priestly authors' own categories, applied with surgical care, make of their own text?**

Applied with that care, the two-goat rite is not one act in two phases. It is two operations sharing a calendar but not a mechanics. The slain goat's blood is a ritual detergent applied inward to the *kappōret*, the inner altar, the outer altar — to *the house*, to scrub off the year's accumulated *ṭum'ah*. The live goat is a load-bearing carrier dispatched outward to *ʾereṣ gəzērâ*, the cut-off land, with *ʿăwonōt* pressed onto its head by verbal confession and a unique two-handed gesture. The first repair holds the house habitable so YHWH does not withdraw. The second lifts the legal-liability burden off the people. Both must be done because they are doing different things to different objects with different verbs.

## b. The Opponents' Strongest Cases

**Monk B's strongest claim** is grammatical and canonical. Verse 5 names "two male goats *lə-ḥaṭṭā't*" — singular noun, both animals — *before* the lots are cast. Verse 10 uses *ləkappēr ʿālāyw* over the live goat, locating its ceremony inside the *kipper*-field. Verse 20's *kallotō mikkappēr* chains the two phases as "when he has finished purging... he shall present the live goat" — continuity-marker rather than seam. Verse 30 describes the day as a unified *kipper* "to cleanse you from all your sins." Sklar's "integrated *kipper*" — purge *and* ransom in one act — claims the verb itself spans both operations. Mishnah Yoma performs the day as one continuous liturgy. Therefore: one rite, two visible movements.

**Monk C's strongest claim** is meta-hermeneutical. Rites are practices, not propositions. The bidirectional choreography — centripetal blood inward, centrifugal body outward — is over-determined by Douglas's pan-ANE inside/outside cosmology. Girard's two-phase mechanism (sacralized victim plus expelled victim) predicts the doubling without theological mechanics. The "two ops or one act" debate is a category error: priestly *theology* is the priestly community's secondary self-description of work that is more fundamentally anthropological. The two-operations philologist and the unified-canonical theologian are both trapped inside the priestly self-description; structural anthropology alone sees the substrate.

These are the strongest forms. Here is precisely why each fails.

## c. Diagnosis of Each Failure

**Monk B fails because it cannot account for the procedural detail's bifurcation of what v. 5 names.** If v. 5's singular *lə-ḥaṭṭā't* controlled ritual identity, vv. 7–22 — the exquisitely careful two-procedure prescription — would be redundant ornamentation. The priestly text does not waste words. When v. 5 names "two male goats *lə-ḥaṭṭā't*" and the procedural detail then prescribes for one: slaughter, blood collection, sevenfold sprinkling on the *kappōret*, daubing on altar horns, sanctuary purgation; and for the other: no slaughter, no blood, double-handed pressing (*shtê yādāyw*, found nowhere else in the priestly cult), verbal confession of *ʿăwonōt* and *pishʿīm* and *ḥaṭṭō'tām*, transfer onto the goat's head, dispatch into wilderness, *naśā'* — when every operational feature of the live-goat procedure deviates from ḥaṭṭā't-procedure, the text is *teaching us how to read v. 5*. Verse 5 is a category-label for the day's required animals, parallel to the multi-animal category-labels of Num 28–29. The procedural detail then bifurcates what v. 5 names. Sklar's "integrated *kipper*" is sophisticated theological conjecture, but it cannot rescue the unified reading from the flat fact that the live-goat procedure is anomalous to every other ḥaṭṭā't in the priestly system. Anomalous because, in its actual mechanics, *it is not a ḥaṭṭā't*. It is an elimination rite folded into the day's ḥaṭṭā't-cluster.

As for v. 10's *ləkappēr ʿālāyw*: this phrase governs the live goat's *standing-before-YHWH* prior to dispatch — its being-set-apart for the elimination operation — not the operation itself. The actual operation, when the priestly text gets to it in vv. 21–22, drops *kipper* entirely and uses *naśā'*. That verb-shift is the seam. Verse 20's *kallotō mikkappēr* — "when he has *finished* the purging" — is, on the priestly text's face, a closure-marker before something different begins.

And the Mishnah, despite the unified reading's appeal to it: even Yoma 3:8 (the high priest's confession over the bull) and Yoma 6:2 (the confession over the live goat) are *linguistically distinguished* — different formulae, different objects, different recipients of the transferred liability. The Mishnah preserves the bifurcation in its very performance. The unification into a single "atonement" theology happens in *later* halakhic and Christian receptions, not in the priestly source.

**Monk C fails because it grants 20th-century anthropological theory more interpretive authority than the priestly text's own self-presentation.** This move is methodologically unjustifiable. The priestly authors built an integrated system in which *ḥaṭṭā't*-blood penetrates to graded depths of the sanctuary corresponding to the gravity of contamination (Lev 4); in which *naśā' ʿāwon* is the consistent idiom for the carriage of legal liability (Lev 5:1, 7:18, 17:16, 19:8, 20:17 — Schwartz's 1995 essay catalogues the distribution); in which the holiness-pollution physics is coherent across hundreds of pages of cultic legislation. To say their theology is a "secondary self-description" of work they did not really understand is the academic equivalent of telling a watchmaker her account of escapements is folk-theology and that anthropology alone sees what watchmaking *really does*.

The two-operations position is not naïve to structural-symbolic resonance. Douglas's centripetal/centrifugal vectors are real and cohere with the two-operations reading; they do not refute it. The inversion is symbolically eloquent — and is *also* doing determinate ritual work the priestly authors specified in determinate language. The comparative ANE field strengthens this point rather than dissolving it: Mesopotamian *kuppuru*-rites (sheep blood used to wipe temple walls in the Akītu) and Hittite elimination rites (Ašḫella, Ambazzi, Šamuḫa, the *nakkušši*-substitutes) are *operationally distinct genres*. The priestly innovation in Lev 16 is to combine them within one liturgical day. Wright's *Disposal of Impurity* (1987) demonstrates this conclusively. The combination is liturgical, not operational.

Girard's scapegoat, finally, is not the priestly text's *ʿăzāzēl*-goat. Girard's scapegoat is a mob-victim onto whom communal violence is projected and sacralized. Lev 16's live goat is not a mob-victim. The people do not project. The priest performs a *formal verbal transfer*. The goat is not killed in the ritual proper. Girard's anthropology may illuminate origins of the ritual genre, but it is not a reading of the priestly text. It is a reading of something the priestly text drew on and then domesticated.

## d. The Deeper Principle: Philological Fidelity

The principle at stake, beneath all the textual sparring, is *philological fidelity to the text the priestly authors actually wrote.* Both opposing positions, in different ways, refuse to take the priestly authors' own categories seriously enough. The canonical reader subordinates the priestly distinctions to a harmonizing theological frame the priestly authors did not endorse. The structural anthropologist subordinates them to an explanatory frame from outside that the priestly authors are denied the right to refuse. Both are versions of the same hermeneutical sin: the refusal to let the priestly authors mean what they said in the words they chose.

The priestly authors chose *kipper* for what blood does to objects and *naśā'* for what a living carrier does with a load. They chose *ṭum'ah* for the contagion that lodges on the sancta and *ʿāwon* for the legal liability that lodges on persons. They chose *haqqōdesh* as the object of *kipper* in vv. 16–19 and *kol-ʿăwonōtām* as the object of *naśā'* in v. 22. *Kipper* takes ritual loci as its objects; *naśā' ʿāwon* takes legal liability as its object. The unified reading must explain away dozens of priestly passages where the verbs do not interchange. It cannot.

## e. The Uncomfortable Form

Here is the thesis in the form that disturbs canonical and devotional readers and ought to: **the priestly authors did not have a doctrine of "atonement" in any post-biblical sense.** They had a doctrine of *sanctuary maintenance* — keep the house clean so YHWH does not withdraw — and a separate doctrine of *iniquity-disposal* — relieve the people of the *ʿāwon*-burden by transferring it to a carrier and dispatching the carrier beyond the boundary of habitable order. The smooshing of these into "atonement" is a post-priestly theological move common to later rabbinic Judaism, Christianity, and modern devotional readings alike, that obscures what the priestly text is actually doing.

Yom Kippur, *as the priestly authors wrote it*, is not "the day God forgives sins." It is the day the house gets scrubbed and the year's iniquity-load gets carried out. Forgiveness is not the priestly text's category. It is the Holiness-school overlay's category (16:30 — *ləṭahēr* "to cleanse you... from all your sins"), and Knohl's stratification has shown 16:30 to be HS editorial pressure pulling the older priestly austerity toward personal-forgiveness language. The PT core does not speak of YHWH forgiving the worshiper. It speaks of the priest purging the sanctuary and the goat bearing iniquities away. The benefit to the worshiper is real but indirect: a clean house in which YHWH continues to dwell, and a community no longer carrying its *ʿăwon*-load. Anything more is theology over the priestly text, not theology of it.

## f. The Reasoning Skeleton

- **Premise 1:** The priestly authors were technical specialists whose terminology is determinate.
- **Premise 2:** *Kipper* and *naśā'* are non-synonymous in priestly usage. *Kipper* takes ritual loci as its objects in cultic contexts; *naśā' ʿāwon* takes legal liability as its object in the priestly idiom Schwartz catalogues.
- **Premise 3:** The objects of *kipper* in 16:16–19 are the sanctuary and the altar, not persons. The grammar is unambiguous: *wəkipper ʿal-haqqōdesh* (16:16); the daubing-and-sprinkling sequence on the altar horns (16:18–19).
- **Premise 4:** The object of *naśā'* in 16:22 is the people's *ʿăwonōt*, transferred to the goat by the unique double-handed pressing and verbal confession of v. 21.
- **Conclusion:** The two procedures address different objects via different verbs and therefore perform different operations.

The load-bearing claim is **Premise 2**. If *kipper* and *naśā'* were essentially synonymous — Kiuchi's claim from Lev 10:17, Sklar's from his integrated-*kipper* model — the bifurcation collapses. They are not synonymous. Lev 10:17 is one passage in which *kipper* and the bearing-of-sin idiom appear in overlapping context, and even there the verbs do different work in the syntax. Against this one passage stand the dozens in which *kipper* governs ritual loci and *naśā' ʿāwon* governs legal liability without overlap. Schwartz's distribution analysis is decisive.

## Thesis

Leviticus 16 prescribes two structurally distinct ritual operations on a single liturgical day: the slain goat's blood purges the sanctuary of *ṭum'ah* so that YHWH continues to indwell, and the live goat bears Israel's *ʿăwon*-burden into the cut-off land so that the people are relieved of legal liability. *Kipper* governs the first, *naśā'* the second; their distributions in priestly usage are not interchangeable; their objects, procedures, and trajectories are inverse. The unified-canonical reading flattens these distinctions into a Christianized "atonement" the priestly text refuses; the structural-anthropological reading relegates them to a secondary self-description the priestly authors are denied the right to assert. Both refuse to let the priestly authors mean what they said. Read on its own terms, with the philological care its authors earned, Yom Kippur is not "the day God forgives sins" — it is the day the house gets scrubbed and the year's iniquity-load gets carried out, and forgiveness, properly speaking, is not the priestly text's category at all.
