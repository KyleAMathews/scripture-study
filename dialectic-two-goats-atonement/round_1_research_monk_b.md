# Round 1 Research Dossier — Monk B
## The Unified-Ritual / Canonical Position on Leviticus 16

> "And he shall take of the congregation of the children of Israel **two kids of the goats for a sin offering** [שְׁנֵי־שְׂעִירֵי עִזִּים לְחַטָּאת], and one ram for a burnt offering." — Leviticus 16:5

> "Aaron shall present the bull as **his** sin offering, and shall make atonement for himself and for his house... and from the congregation of the people of Israel he shall take **two male goats for a sin offering**..." — Lev 16:5-6

The text itself names the two goats — singular, collective — *one* ḥaṭṭā't. That is the load-bearing exegetical fact this dossier defends. Everything else — the lots, the blood, the wilderness, the confession, the date — is the unfolding of a single ritual logic.

---

## 1. The Verse 5 Argument: One Ḥaṭṭā't, Two Movements

The Hebrew of 16:5 is decisive and the modern critical tradition has consistently understood it without consistently weighing it. The phrase reads:

> שְׁנֵי־שְׂעִירֵי עִזִּים **לְחַטָּאת** (`šᵉnê-śᵉʿîrê ʿizzîm lᵉḥaṭṭā't`)

Note carefully: not לְחַטָּאוֹת (plural, "for sin offerings") but לְחַטָּאת — singular, with the lamed of purpose. Two animals, one purpose, one offering. The grammar is not ambiguous and not incidental. The Priestly writer, who is the most careful and lexically precise editor in the Hebrew Bible, names this rite with intentional singularity. As one widely-cited summary puts the point: "Two kids of the goats as a [singular] sin offering... Although two goats were to be presented before the Eternal, Scripture makes it clear that there would be only one sin offering — singular rather than plural" (https://www.theberean.org/index.cfm/main/default/id/10072/leviticus-16-5.htm).

**This is the Achilles heel of the bifurcating reading.** Milgrom and his school treat the two goats as performing two semantically distinct operations: blood-goat purges the sanctuary; scapegoat removes Israel's sin. But the Priestly writer himself — the same writer Milgrom credits with surgical lexical precision elsewhere — refuses to lexically distinguish them. They are *one* ḥaṭṭā't.

Kiuchi (*The Purification Offering in the Priestly Literature*, JSOT Sup. 56, 1987) saw this clearly. As summarized in Themelios's review of his work: "the two goats offered comprise one ḥaṭṭā't, and the expulsion of the scapegoat is equivalent to the burning of the ḥaṭṭā't outside the camp, symbolizing the removal of guilt" (https://www.thegospelcoalition.org/themelios/review/the-purification-offering-in-the-priestly-literature-its-meaning-and-function/). For Kiuchi, the live goat sent to Azazel is not a *separate* rite added to the sin offering — it is the disposal-phase of the singular ḥaṭṭā't, structurally parallel to the bull's carcass being burned outside the camp in Lev 4 (cf. Lev 4:11-12, 21).

This is the canonical reading's first move and it is grammatically anchored: **the text refuses to separate what scholars want to separate.**

## 2. The Ritual Flow as a Single Integrated Logic

Read Leviticus 16 not as a composite of distinct cultic operations but as one performance with internal stages. The chapter's own structure resists fragmentation:

- **16:3-5** — Aaron prepares: bull (his own sin offering), two goats (the people's *single* sin offering), ram (burnt offering).
- **16:6-10** — The selection by lot: both goats are *presented together* "before the LORD at the door of the tent of meeting" (v.7). They stand together as a unit. The lot then determines *function within the unit*, not *separation into two units*.
- **16:11-19** — Blood ritual: Aaron slaughters his bull, then the LORD's goat, sprinkling blood inside the Holy of Holies, on the tent, on the altar. This stage purges holy space of the people's defilement.
- **16:20-22** — Scapegoat: "When he has made an end of *atoning* (כַּלֹּתוֹ מִכַּפֵּר) for the holy place..." (v.20) — the verb is *kipper*, the same verb used for what was just done with blood. Aaron now lays both hands on the live goat, confesses, sends it away. The scapegoat phase is not described as a *different* atoning act; it is described as continuous with what came before. Verse 20 explicitly chains them: he completes (כָּלָה) the kipper of the sanctuary *and then* turns to the live goat — the live goat does not begin a separate atoning operation; it concludes the same one.
- **16:29-34** — The statute fixes the day annually as *the* day of atonement (יוֹם הַכִּפֻּרִים — note the plural intensive, not because there are multiple atonements but because the day's atonement is comprehensive).

**Why the same priest?** Because it is one priest performing one duty. If sanctuary-purgation and people-cleansing were two ritual categories, the Priestly writer (who elsewhere distinguishes priestly grades meticulously) would not have lashed them so tightly together that the high priest must do both, on one day, in one continuous performance, vested in the same linen garments (16:23 — he changes only after both phases are complete, again signaling continuous ritual identity).

**Why the same day?** Because Yom Kippur is not "a day on which two rites happen to occur." It is *the* atonement day, and the rite that defines it is the integrated handling of sin in its full structural reality.

The chapter's redactional shape — bracketed by Aaron's death-warning (v.1-2) and the perpetual-statute closure (v.29-34) — frames the whole as a single covenantal provision, not a compendium.

## 3. Sklar's Integrated Kipper: The Decisive Modern Counter to Milgrom

This is the single most important contemporary argument for the unified position. Jay Sklar, in *Sin, Impurity, Sacrifice, Atonement: The Priestly Conceptions* (Hebrew Bible Monographs 2; Sheffield Phoenix, 2005), broke the Milgromian binary by demonstrating from the priestly texts themselves that **kipper is integrated, not bifurcated**.

Milgrom argued: kipper means *purgation* of sanctuary in impurity contexts, but a different mechanism (substitutionary sin-bearing) operates in sin contexts. Sklar's response is sharper and more textually grounded:

> "Inadvertent sin and major impurity share in common the property of *endangering* (thus requiring ransom) and *polluting* (thus requiring purgation)." (Summary in Themelios review: https://www.thegospelcoalition.org/themelios/review/sin-impurity-sacrifice-atonement-the-priestly-conceptions/)

Sklar's thesis, developed under Gordon Wenham at Cheltenham:

1. **Sin endangers AND defiles.** Inadvertent sin is not just a forensic problem (debt) but a metaphysical one (pollution). It pollutes the sanctuary while also placing the sinner in mortal danger.
2. **Impurity defiles AND endangers.** Major impurities don't just mark someone unclean; they expose them to sanctions including death.
3. **Therefore kipper, in a single integrated act, both *ransoms* (kopher-language) and *purifies*.** The priestly literature uses *one* verb for *one* integrated reality.
4. **Lev 17:11 — "for the life [nepheš] of the flesh is in the blood, and I have given it to you upon the altar to make atonement [לְכַפֵּר] for your souls; for it is the blood that makes atonement (יְכַפֵּר) by reason of the life."** This is Sklar's load-bearing verse. Life-bearing blood does *both* in a single mechanism: it ransoms (life-for-life) and it purifies (decontaminates by virtue of life-against-death).

The decisive blow to Milgrom: Sklar shows that the same kipper act has the same blood doing the same work — and the bifurcating reading must artificially split what the priestly writer welded together. The binary is not in the text; it is imposed by a hermeneutic that mistakes ritual specialization for ritual fragmentation.

**For Leviticus 16, this means:** the goat for YHWH (blood ritual) and the goat for Azazel (sin-removal ritual) are not two different mechanisms doing two different jobs. They are the integrated kipper-act in two visible modes. Blood ransoms-and-purifies *inside* the sanctuary; the live goat ransoms-and-purifies *outside* it by physically conveying the sin away. **One mechanism, two performances. One ḥaṭṭā't, two phases.**

Sklar's *Leviticus* in the ZECOT series (Zondervan, 2023) carries this through to Leviticus 16 explicitly: the rite is a single integrated handling of Israel's full sin-condition.

## 4. The Mishnah Yoma — Second Temple Judaism Knew the Rite Was One

This matters enormously because the Mishnah was compiled by Jews whose priests had actually performed Yom Kippur. They were closer to the ritual *in performance* than any modern critical scholar, and they did not fragment it.

**Yoma 4–6** describes the high priest's continuous performance. The structure:

- Yoma 3:8 — high priest's first confession over the *bull* (his own and his house's sins).
- Yoma 4:1-2 — the lots are cast on the two goats.
- Yoma 4:2 — a *crimson thread* is tied to the head of the scapegoat and another to the throat of the LORD's goat. **The thread is one ritual mark** signifying that both goats belong to one rite.
- Yoma 5 — high priest enters the Holy of Holies with blood.
- Yoma 6:2 — high priest's confession over the live goat: "O God, Your people the house of Israel have sinned, transgressed, rebelled before You. O God, **grant atonement** (כַּפֶּר־נָא) for the sins, transgressions, rebellions..." (https://www.sefaria.org/Mishnah_Yoma.6.2). Note: the verb is *kipper*. The same verb. The same atonement. Now invoked over the live goat.
- Yoma 6:6 — when the goat reaches the wilderness, *messengers signal back to the high priest* so he can continue his ritual. **This is the Mishnah's most striking testimony to ritual unity:** the high priest's rite cannot proceed to its conclusion until the goat is gone. The two phases are interlocked, not parallel.
- Yoma 6:8 — only when the goat has been driven off does the high priest begin the public Torah reading and the closing offerings.

**The Mishnah's structural witness:** the entire day is one continuous performance, narrated by the rabbis as one liturgy. The same priest speaks the same atonement-prayer over both animals (with the bull confession, the LORD's goat is implicitly included; the scapegoat receives the explicit confession of "iniquities, transgressions, sins" — `ʿăwōnōt, pᵉšāʿîm, ḥaṭṭā'îm`). Atonement-language attaches to the day as a whole and to *both* movements.

If anyone in history was qualified to bifurcate the rite into "sanctuary purgation" vs. "sin removal," it was the priests who actually did it. They didn't.

## 5. Hebrews 9–10 — Canonical Witness to the Rite's Unity

Hebrews is not a Christian *imposition* on Leviticus; in canonical reading it is a *witness within scripture* to how the rite is integrated. The author of Hebrews was a Jew of priestly literacy reading Yom Kippur under inspiration, and he reads it as one type fulfilled by one act.

**Hebrews 9:7** — names the day: "but into the second [tent] only the high priest goes, and *he but once a year, and not without taking blood which he offers for himself and for the unintentional sins of the people*." Note: blood (the LORD's goat phase) is named as offered "for the unintentional sins of the people" — exactly the function the bifurcating reading wants to assign to the *scapegoat* alone. Hebrews collapses the supposed distinction at v.7.

**Hebrews 9:11-12** — Christ "entered once for all into the Holy Places, not by means of the blood of goats and calves but by means of his own blood, thus securing an eternal redemption." Singular Holy Place, singular entrance, one redemption — and the typological referent is the unified Yom Kippur rite.

**Hebrews 9:13-14** — "For if the blood of goats and bulls, and the sprinkling of defiled persons with the ashes of a heifer, *sanctify for the purification of the flesh*, how much more will the blood of Christ... purify our conscience..." Hebrews here ascribes to *Levitical blood* the *people-purifying* function that Milgrom restricts to other channels. Hebrews knows what the rite was for: the people, integrally.

**Hebrews 13:11-13** — The capstone. "For the bodies of those animals whose blood is brought into the holy places by the high priest as a sacrifice for sin are *burned outside the camp*. So Jesus also suffered outside the gate in order to sanctify the people through his own blood." This text fuses (a) the blood-ritual goat (whose body was burned outside, Lev 16:27) and (b) the scapegoat (sent outside, Lev 16:21-22) into a *single typological figure*: the single Christ, suffering outside the camp once, fulfills both. Hebrews does not say "Christ fulfills the blood goat AND, separately, Christ fulfills the scapegoat." It says: Christ, outside the camp, in one act, fulfills the rite. The author has already read the rite as one.

**Hebrews 10:10-14** — "by that will we have been sanctified through *the offering of the body of Jesus Christ once for all*... by *a single offering* (μιᾷ προσφορᾷ) he has perfected for all time those who are being sanctified." The Greek is emphatic: ONE offering. If the Levitical type were genuinely two operations, the typological logic of Hebrews 10 would be incoherent. It is coherent precisely because the type was already one.

This is canonical testimony. To dismiss it as "Christian imposition" is to refuse to read scripture as scripture. It is also exegetically wrong: Hebrews demonstrably engages the text of Leviticus 16 in detail, and what it sees there is unity.

## 6. Levenson and the Holistic Logic of Priestly Worship

Jon D. Levenson, throughout *The Death and Resurrection of the Beloved Son* (Yale, 1993) and *Resurrection and the Restoration of Israel* (Yale, 2006), resists the modern critical habit of dissecting biblical rituals into separable mechanical operations. His core hermeneutical move is to treat priestly theology as a *coherent religious vision* rather than a code of bureaucratic procedures.

For Levenson, sin is not one thing — it is **guilt + defilement + bearing**. The priestly worldview does not chop these apart. Guilt (`ʿāwōn`) and pollution (`ṭuməʾâ`) and the burden that must be carried (`nāśā' ʿāwōn`) are *aspects* of one sin-reality, and a unified atoning rite must address them in their integration.

Levenson's claim that bears most directly on Leviticus 16: **rituals that look "doubled" to modern eyes are typically integrated by an inner logic that the modern bureaucratic imagination is constitutionally bad at seeing.** The *aqedah*, Passover, the firstborn-substitution complex — Levenson reads these as integrated narrative-ritual wholes whose meaning evaporates if you fragment them into discrete "operations."

Applied to Yom Kippur: the rite addresses sin's *threefold structure*:
- **Defilement** — answered by blood inside (the LORD's goat).
- **Guilt/debt** — answered by ransom-blood (kopher-function of the same blood).
- **Bearing** — answered by physical removal (the scapegoat's wilderness journey).

But these are not three rites. They are three aspects of one sin-reality answered by one integrated atonement-act. Pulling them apart is precisely the move Levenson resists across his whole corpus: it imposes Cartesian separability on a Hebraic religious imagination that did not work that way.

**The modern critical move treats sin as a problem in three categories needing three handlings. The priestly text treats sin as one problem with three faces needing one handling that displays itself in three ways.**

## 7. Why Two Visible Movements? — Display and Pedagogical Fullness

The strongest version of the unified position must answer: *if it is one rite, why does it visibly have two movements?* The answer is theological-pedagogical.

**The rite must display what atonement IS.** Atonement is not a single point-event but a structured reality. Israel needs to *see*:

- (a) **Blood for life** — the costliness, the substitution, the death-for-life exchange that ransoms and purifies. This is the LORD's goat: blood enters the sanctuary.
- (b) **Removal of sin from the camp** — the visible, narratable, pictorial fact that sin is *gone*, taken out, no longer here. This is the scapegoat: confession, transfer, wilderness, distance.

A rite that did only (a) would teach blood-cost but not removal — Israel would know sin had been answered but not seen it carried away. A rite that did only (b) would teach removal but not the cost — sin would be banished without the death that makes its banishment possible. **Both are necessary to display the integrated reality.**

This is why Calvin, in his Leviticus commentary, reads the two goats as together forming the single sacrifice that prefigures Christ: "an innocent animal should be substituted in the place of men, to be exposed to the curse of God... another means, therefore, of making atonement to God was revealed when Christ, 'being made a curse for us,' transferred to Himself the sins which alienated men from God." For Calvin, the two goats are not two prefigurations of two divine acts; they are the doubled visible face of one prefiguration: the single Christ-event, displayed in two complementary aspects so the worshipper can see what is happening.

Wenham (NICOT *Leviticus*, 1979) similarly reads the rite as integrated: the dual role of the goats — one to die, one to bear away — "creates a literary and theological balance that reinforces the completeness of God's atonement." Note the word *completeness*: it is one atonement, made *complete* by being displayed in both aspects, not two atonements added together.

**Pedagogical fullness, not ritual duplication.** The rite is one; its display is two-fold so that Israel can see it whole.

## 8. Response to Opponents

### 8a. To Milgrom's Sanctuary-Purgation / Sin-Bearer Distinction

Milgrom's textual data is largely correct. The blood ritual *is* directed at sancta. The scapegoat *does* carry sin away. The unified position **accepts the textual data** and rejects only the dualistic *interpretation* of those data.

The critical move: distinguishing *function within a rite* from *separation into rites*. Milgrom slides from "the blood does X here, the scapegoat does Y there" to "therefore X and Y are different operations." This is a non-sequitur. A single integrated rite can perform multiple functions through multiple instruments without being multiple rites. A surgeon's operation involves cutting, suction, and suturing; these are not three operations.

Furthermore: Milgrom's strict bifurcation cannot explain (a) the singular ḥaṭṭā't of v.5, (b) the use of *kipper* over the *live* goat in v.10 ("to make atonement over it" — לְכַפֵּר עָלָיו), (c) the chained continuity of v.20 (the high priest "completes" the kipper of the sanctuary and then turns to the live goat *as the next phase of the same act*), (d) Mishnah Yoma's continuous performance, or (e) Hebrews's typological fusion. Milgrom has to explain *all* of these as either incidental, redactional, or interpretive overlay. The unified reading explains all of them as the surface of one underlying reality.

**The unified reading absorbs Milgrom's data; Milgrom cannot absorb the unified reading's data.**

### 8b. To Anthropological-Structural Readings (Douglas, Girard)

Mary Douglas's *Leviticus as Literature* (Oxford, 1999) and Girard's mimetic reading both bracket theological meaning to expose ritual structure. Douglas reads Leviticus as a coded body-temple-cosmos analogy; Girard reads the scapegoat as the founding mechanism of victimage and social peace.

The unified position's response: these readings can yield genuine insight into the ritual's *form*, but they systematically refuse the question of what the priestly writer himself was doing — namely, articulating Israel's covenant relationship with YHWH in terms of sin, atonement, holiness, and presence. To bracket theology is to misread the genre. Leviticus is not anthropology disguised as religion; it is theology that uses the registers of body, space, and ritual because Hebrew theological imagination is incarnational, not abstract.

Girard in particular forces the scapegoat into a violence-management framework that *Leviticus 16 explicitly resists*: the scapegoat is **not killed by the community**. It is sent away alive (in the biblical text — the cliff is later, halakhic). Girard's mechanism requires collective violence; the priestly rite refuses it. The text is doing something other than what Girard's theory requires it to be doing.

### 8c. To the Christian-Imposition Charge

Critics will say: the unified reading is a Christian retrojection from Hebrews onto Leviticus. The response:
1. The unified reading is *grammatically* anchored in Lev 16:5 — pre-Christian, pre-Hebrews, sitting in the priestly text itself.
2. The unified reading is *liturgically* anchored in Mishnah Yoma — Jewish, post-temple, independently witnessing the rite as one performance.
3. Hebrews then reads what was already there. It is not imposition; it is reception.
4. Calling it "imposition" is itself the imposition of a modern critical hermeneutic that privileges fragmentation as more "objective." Fragmentation is not neutrality; it is a hermeneutic with its own theological content.

## 9. Honest Concessions — Where the Unified Reading Must Work Hard

A serious unified reading must absorb genuine difficulties without flattening them.

### 9a. The Naśā' Problem

Lev 16:21-22 introduces *naśā'* (נשׂא, "to bear, lift, carry"): "the goat shall *bear* (וְנָשָׂא) all their iniquities upon him to a solitary land." This is *not* the typical lexical neighborhood of *kipper*. The bifurcating reading seizes on this: different verb, different operation.

**The unified response:** *naśā' ʿāwōn* and *kipper* are not synonyms but they are not separate operations either. They name *what happens* (sin is borne away) and *the rite that accomplishes it* (atonement). One can describe surgery as "cutting" or "healing" without these being two surgeries. Indeed, the priestly literature elsewhere fuses the two: in Lev 10:17 the priests are told the sin offering was given "to *bear* (לָשֵׂאת) the iniquity of the congregation, *to make atonement* (לְכַפֵּר) for them before the LORD." There the *same offering* both bears and atones — the verbs are coordinate aspects of one act, not labels for two acts. Lev 16:21 is doing exactly the same thing: the live goat *bears* what the rite *atones for*, and the bearing is *how* this phase of the kipper-rite accomplishes the atonement.

Kiuchi's contribution here is essential: he argues that *kipper* in priestly literature *includes* sin-bearing as one of its constituent aspects. The priest, by performing the rite, bears the people's guilt; the live goat completes that bearing visibly by carrying it out. **Naśā' is not a competing concept; it is a constituent of kipper.**

### 9b. Spatial Separation — One at the Altar, One in the Wilderness

The bifurcating reading: surely if one goat is killed at the altar and the other walks into the desert, these are spatially distinct events and therefore distinct operations.

**The unified response:** Spatial separation is *required* by the rite's display logic (point 7 above). To show that sin is removed from the camp, something must visibly *leave* the camp. To show that life-for-life ransom occurs, blood must enter the holiest place. These are spatial requirements of a single atonement that addresses both "before the LORD" (kapporet, sanctuary) and "outside the camp" (wilderness, midbar). The rite's geography mirrors its theological structure: sin must be answered both vertically (toward God's presence) and horizontally (away from the camp). One rite, two geographic vectors.

Compare Lev 4: the bull's blood is sprinkled inside; its body is burned outside. Two locations, one offering. Nobody (not even Milgrom) reads Lev 4's bull as two sacrifices because of spatial movement. The same logic applies to Lev 16's pair: spatial distribution within the rite is not fragmentation of the rite.

### 9c. The Lot

Why cast lots if the goats are one? Doesn't the lot *create* the distinction?

**Unified response:** The lot does not separate; it *assigns roles within the unit*. The two goats are *already* one ḥaṭṭā't (v.5) before the lot is cast (v.8). The lot only tells the priest *which goat will perform which phase*. This is functional differentiation within a unified rite, not the creation of two rites. Compare: a director casts roles in one play. The casting does not create two plays; it specifies who plays what within one performance.

### 9d. The Live Goat Is "Not Killed"

If both are part of one ḥaṭṭā't, why does only one die? Doesn't the lack of bloodshed for the live goat mean it is functionally *not* a sacrifice?

**Unified response:** The disposal of the ḥaṭṭā't is not always blood-shedding. In Lev 4, the bull's body is *burned* outside. In other contexts, the priest *eats* the offering (Lev 10:17). Disposal mode varies; what makes something part of the ḥaṭṭā't is its identification *as* ḥaṭṭā't in the rite's structure. Lev 16:5 has identified both. The live goat's disposal is its expulsion. Kiuchi's analogy: the scapegoat's wilderness is structurally what the burning-outside-the-camp is for other ḥaṭṭā't bulls. **It is the same ritual logic with a different but equivalent disposal mode**, dictated by the rite's pedagogical need to *display* removal.

## 10. Summary — The Position the Monk Must Inhabit

**Central thesis:** Leviticus 16 is one ritual, named one ḥaṭṭā't by the text itself (v.5), performed by one priest on one day in one continuous liturgical sequence, addressing sin in its full structural reality (defilement + guilt + bearing) through a single atoning act displayed in two visible movements (blood-into-sanctuary + sin-into-wilderness).

**Lexical anchor:** לְחַטָּאת (singular, v.5); kipper applied to both phases (v.10, v.20, v.30); naśā' as constituent of kipper (Lev 10:17).

**Mechanism:** Sklar's integrated kipper — life-bearing blood ransoms-and-purifies in one act; the live goat completes the rite by physically conveying the sin-burden out of the camp, displaying what the blood has accomplished.

**Witnesses:** Mishnah Yoma (Jewish liturgical reception); Hebrews 9–10 and 13:11-13 (canonical New Testament reception fusing both goats into one Christ-act); Calvin (Reformation theological reception); Childs, Wenham, Sklar, Kiuchi, Levenson (modern canonical/integrated readings).

**Against Milgrom:** accept the textual data on differentiated function; reject the move from differentiated function to separate operations. The text refuses to lexically split what Milgrom interpretively splits.

**Against Douglas/Girard:** anthropological-structural readings illuminate form but bracket the text's own theological self-presentation; they cannot account for the priestly writer's covenantal frame.

**On hard cases:** *naśā'* is a constituent of *kipper*, not its rival (Lev 10:17 fuses them); spatial separation is a display-requirement of the rite, not evidence of fragmentation; the lot assigns roles within a unit, not separation into units; differential disposal modes (death vs. expulsion) are standard in priestly ritual and do not split offerings.

**Final formulation:** *The two goats are the two faces of one atonement, because sin has more than one face and yet is one reality.* To split them is to misread the priestly imagination. To unify them is to read scripture as scripture intends to be read — canonically, integrally, theologically, doxologically.
