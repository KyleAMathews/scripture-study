# Round 1 Research — Monk C: The Anthropological / Structural Position

*A research dossier for the Electric Monk arguing that the "two operations vs. one act" debate over Leviticus 16 is a category error. Theological mechanics are downstream of the ritual's prior structural and anthropological work: cosmic boundary maintenance and the management of communal violence.*

---

## 0. The Frame Both Sides Are Inside

Before any argument: the monk must internalize the move. The Milgrom-style priestly-theological reading and the canonical-unified reading are *both* treating Leviticus 16 as if it were a *text with semantic content* — a coded theological proposition awaiting decoding. Did the slain goat purify the sanctuary while the live goat carried away iniquity (two operations)? Or did both goats together effect a single unified act of atonement? Both camps are arguing about *what the rite means*.

The anthropological/structural position interrupts: a rite is not primarily a proposition. A rite is a practice. It does not *encode* theological content, it *produces* social and cosmic effects — it forms a people, restores a world, manages violence, and maintains boundaries. Asking "what does it mean theologically" is like asking what a cathedral *says* rather than what a cathedral *does* to the bodies that move through it.

The monk's task is not to refute the theologians — it is to step around behind them and point out the wall they're both pressed against.

---

## 1. Mary Douglas: The Tabernacle as Cosmological Diagram

### 1.1 The basic move from *Purity and Danger* (1966)

Douglas's foundational insight in *Purity and Danger* is that pollution is not a moral or hygienic category but a *structural* one: "dirt is matter out of place." Dirt presupposes a system of classification; it is the residue of a culture's effort to impose order on an "inherently untidy experience." Famously: "Holiness means keeping distinct the categories of creation. It therefore involves correct definition, discrimination and order." Sin, for the priestly mind, is not a moral substance but a *boundary violation* — the wrong thing in the wrong zone.

This reframes everything about Leviticus from the start. The hattat ("sin offering") is mistranslated; Milgrom himself moved toward "purification offering." But Douglas presses further: the rite is not about removing a *substance* (whether moral guilt or ritual pollution as quasi-physical contagion) — it is about *restoring a classificatory order* that has slipped.

### 1.2 *Leviticus as Literature* (1999): the book IS the tabernacle

Douglas's late masterwork makes a radical claim: the literary structure of Leviticus mirrors the tripartite spatial structure of the tabernacle, which itself mirrors Mount Sinai, which itself mirrors the cosmos and the human body. The book is a *spatial diagram in narrative form*.

The three zones:
- **Chapters 1–17** = the Outer Court (sacrifices, purity laws, the everyday work of the people's holiness)
- **Chapters 18–24** = the Holy Place (priestly rules, ethical legislation, the sanctuary's middle zone)
- **Chapters 25–27** = the Holy of Holies (covenant, jubilee, the framing "on Mount Sinai")

The two narrative interruptions — the Nadab/Abihu story (Lev 8–10) and the blasphemer narrative (Lev 24:10–23) — function as the *two screens* (the *masak* curtain dividing court from Holy Place; the *paroket* dividing Holy Place from Holy of Holies). The book is a *virtual tour* of sacred space; reading it is liturgical movement through ordered zones.

The takeaway for the monk: for Douglas, Leviticus does not *talk about* the cosmos. It *is* a cosmos. The rituals it prescribes are not symbols of theological transactions but operations within a built-up classificatory architecture. To grasp a rite, you must grasp the *zones* it traverses.

### 1.3 The two goats as a spatial operation, not a theological transaction

Apply Douglas's spatial logic to Leviticus 16. There are exactly three zones: the Holy of Holies (the divine throne, the *kapporet*), the camp (the ordered human community), and the wilderness (chaos, the un-cultivated, the realm beyond classification). The Day of Atonement choreography moves *blood* and *animal bodies* through these zones in a specific pattern.

- **Goat 1 (the Lord's lot):** its blood is carried *inward*, through screen after screen, into the deepest interior — the *kapporet*. Its trajectory is centripetal: a substance moves from outside-in toward the cosmic center.
- **Goat 2 (Azazel's lot):** the goat's body is driven *outward*, through the camp, beyond the boundary, into the wilderness. Its trajectory is centrifugal: a substance moves from inside-out toward the cosmic periphery.

This is not "two operations" or "one act" in any theological sense. It is a *single spatial operation* — the simultaneous re-articulation of inside and outside. Both vectors are required for boundary maintenance: you cannot have an inside without an outside, and the rite *re-stages* the distinction by moving things in both directions at once.

The monk should be insistent: *the structural fact is the choreography, not the meaning.* Whether you call it "purgation" or "atonement" or "transfer of sin," the rite *does* the same thing — it re-establishes the topology of the holy.

### 1.4 Azazel and the wilderness: where disorder belongs

For Douglas, the wilderness in Israel's spatial imagination is not "a place where God isn't" but the realm of unstructured chaos, the un-cultivated, the demonic-residual. Whether "Azazel" denotes a wilderness demon (as in 1 Enoch's recasting), a goat-deity, a place name, or an abstract noun ("complete removal"), the function is the same: it names *the not-camp*, the zone outside classification.

The crucial reframe: sending the goat into the wilderness is *not a transfer of sin to a recipient*. There is no recipient in any meaningful sense. It is a *return of disorder to where disorder belongs*. The wilderness does not *receive* sin the way a creditor receives a payment; the wilderness *is* where disorder lives, and the goat is the vehicle by which disordering material is repatriated to its proper zone.

This dissolves the long debate about whether Azazel is a person, demon, place, or abstraction. From Douglas's spatial logic, the question is malformed. *Azazel is whatever names the outside.*

## 2. René Girard: The Scapegoat as Anthropological Substrate

### 2.1 Mimetic desire and the founding murder

Girard's anthropology begins with mimesis. Humans desire what others desire — desire is mediated, not autonomous. Mimetic desire produces rivalry. Rivalry, multiplied across a community, escalates into a *crisis of undifferentiation*: as rivals come to mirror each other, distinctions collapse, and the community spirals toward reciprocal violence that would tear it apart.

The *scapegoat mechanism* is Girard's name for the spontaneous resolution: at the peak of crisis, the violence finds an arbitrary victim, fixes on them with sudden unanimity, and the collective expulsion or killing discharges the contagion. Peace returns. The victim — having "caused" both the crisis (in being killed for it) and the peace (by their death) — is retrospectively divinized. This is, for Girard, the *foundation of culture*: religion, ritual, prohibition, and myth all emerge as the community's attempt to repeat and stabilize the original peace-restoring murder.

### 2.2 Ritual as controlled re-enactment

Ritual sacrifice is the *calendrically managed, sublimated form* of what was once a spontaneous mob act. The community no longer spirals into actual violence; instead, at fixed intervals, it rehearses the founding murder with a designated victim — animal or human — and reaps the same prophylactic effect. As Girard puts it in *Violence and the Sacred*: sacrifice "polarizes" maleficent violence onto the victim and "metamorphoses" it into beneficent violence by the victim's death or expulsion. "The sacred is 'bad' when it is inside the community, it is 'good' when it returns to the exterior."

The phrase that should ring in the monk's head: **sacrificial ritual is the technology by which a community manages its own internal violence by exporting it.**

### 2.3 The two goats as two phases of one mechanism

This is Girard's most provocative move, and the monk should press it hard. The two goats of Leviticus 16 are not two separate operations. They are *two phases of the single anthropological mechanism*, ritually distinguished:

- **The slain goat** is the *sacrificial* form: a sanctioned victim is killed; the violence is enacted but bounded; the blood (Girard would say: the residue of sacralized killing) consecrates the community's center.
- **The live scapegoat** is the *anthropologically older* form: the community projects collective guilt onto a bearer, drives the bearer out, and peace returns. The expulsion is the more primitive, more transparent form of the mechanism — it lacks even the masking element of "this is a holy slaughter for God."

The brilliance of Lev 16 — and what Girard finds anthropologically revealing — is that it preserves *both* forms side by side, doubled, on the same day. As if the priestly redactors knew (or sensed without knowing) that the mechanism has these two faces, and ritualized both.

The unity of the rite, on this reading, is not theological-textual ("one act of atonement"). It is anthropological-mechanistic ("one mimetic discharge in two ritual phases"). This precisely answers the canonical-unified reading: yes, there is one act, but the act is not theological atonement, it is the discharge of communal violence through sanctioned scapegoating.

### 2.4 Reveal and conceal: the priestly text as transitional

In *Things Hidden Since the Foundation of the World* (1978), Girard argues the Bible *progressively reveals* the scapegoat mechanism that all other religions conceal. Myth tells the story from the perspective of the perpetrator-community ("the victim was guilty; killing them restored peace; therefore they were divine"). The Hebrew Bible begins to invert this: from Abel onward, the victim's innocence becomes legible; the prophets denounce sacrifice; Job protests his persecution; the Suffering Servant of Isaiah 53 makes the innocent victim's perspective central; and the Gospels complete the unmasking by narrating the Passion from the victim's standpoint and declaring him innocent.

Leviticus 16 sits in the *middle* of this trajectory. It is *transitional*. The priestly text has not yet broken the mechanism — it still ritualizes it, still uses it for community formation. But the doubling of the goats, the explicit confession over the live goat, the precise calendrical containment — these are signs that the mechanism is becoming *visible to itself* in a way it was not in pre-biblical sacrificial systems. The text simultaneously *uses* the mechanism (as a working priestly technology) and *exposes* it (by ritualizing it so transparently).

The monk should put it this way: *Leviticus 16 is the scapegoat mechanism almost-but-not-yet making itself legible.* The theology of kipper is the priestly rationalization laid over the top — true to its own internal logic, but secondary to the anthropological substrate it manages.

### 2.5 Specific Leviticus 16 reading

Girard reads the rite this way: the people accumulate mimetic guilt across the year. On the appointed day, the high priest performs a controlled discharge. Confession over the live goat is the formal act of *projection* — the community's accumulated rivalry-residue is loaded onto the victim. The expulsion into the wilderness completes the discharge. The sanctuary, freshly purged of the year's accumulated violence-residue, can resume its function as cosmic center. The community, having vicariously enacted its founding murder, is bound together for another cycle.

The genius of the priestly system is that it *prevents* the mob act by ritualizing it. The cost is honesty: the mechanism remains covert, dressed in theological vocabulary (kipper, hattat, awon). The Christ-event, on Girard's reading, finally rips off the dress.

## 3. Catherine Bell: Ritualization Produces, It Doesn't Encode

Catherine Bell's *Ritual Theory, Ritual Practice* (1992) is the methodological backbone the monk needs to deflect "but what does it *mean*?" objections.

### 3.1 Ritual is not a code

Bell's central polemical target is what she calls the *meaning paradigm* — the assumption (shared by Frazer, Eliade, much of comparative religion, and most theological exegesis) that rituals are *symbolic statements* whose proper analysis is decoding. She argues this view is methodologically catastrophic because it presupposes an artificial split between "thought" (the meaning) and "action" (the rite as mere vehicle), then reduces the action to a footnote on the thought.

Real rituals do not work that way. They are not pictures of beliefs. They are practices that *produce* the dispositions, the bodies, the social subjects, and the perceived realities that propositional theology will *later* try to articulate.

### 3.2 Ritualization and the ritualized body

Bell coins *ritualization* — the strategic differentiation of certain actions from ordinary ones, so as to produce a particular kind of agent. Drawing on Bourdieu's *habitus* and *practical mastery*, she introduces *ritual mastery*: the embodied, pre-reflective competence that ritual cultivates. The "ritualized body" carries what it has come to possess in ritual *into ordinary social life*. The schemas embedded by ritualization come to *feel like* the structure of reality itself.

Apply this to Leviticus 16. The rite is not delivering a theological proposition about how God forgives. The rite is *producing* Israelites — bodies that have stood at the camp boundary watching the goat depart, bodies that have heard the high priest pronounce the divine name, bodies that have held the year's calendar around this anchor. After the rite, the participant *is* an Israelite of a particular ordered kind, with the cosmos felt as ordered, with sin felt as exportable, with the camp felt as holy. The rite has done its work whether or not anyone could correctly state a theology of kipper.

### 3.3 The methodological consequence

If Bell is right, then the entire Milgrom-vs-canonical debate is operating in the wrong register. It is asking *what proposition the rite encodes*. But rites do not encode propositions; rites *form people and worlds*. The right question is: *what kind of Israel does this rite produce, and what cosmos does it leave behind once a year?*

Answer (combining Douglas + Bell): it produces a people with an embodied sense of holy distinctions, ordered zones, and the regular maintenance of the boundary between camp and wilderness. The rite does this whether the theologians say "two operations" or "one act."

## 4. Burkert and Hubert/Mauss: Comparative Sacrificial Anthropology

### 4.1 Burkert: *Homo Necans* (1972)

Walter Burkert's *Homo Necans* — "Man the Killer" — argues that sacrificial ritual originates in the paleolithic hunt and the guilt of intra-species aggression turned outward. Sacrifice is *ritualized aggression*: the community comes into being by jointly killing, and reaffirms its bond by repeating that killing under controlled conditions. "The guilt incurred in the violence of the hunt was reflected in sacred crimes, which through rituals of cleansing and expiation served to unify communities."

Burkert's frame complements Girard: where Girard locates the founding violence in mimetic crisis and victim-discharge, Burkert locates it in the hunt and the management of aggression. Both converge on: *sacrifice is a community-binding technology built around violence.* The "what does atonement do" question, on Burkert's reading, is anachronistic — the rite predates and undergirds the theology.

For Lev 16: the ritual is recognizable across the ancient Mediterranean as a community-binding sanctioned-killing event. The Israelite priestly framework gives it a particular monotheistic theology, but the underlying form is a near-universal pattern.

### 4.2 Hubert and Mauss: *Sacrifice: Its Nature and Function* (1898)

Hubert and Mauss define sacrifice as "a religious act which, through the consecration of a victim, modifies the condition of the moral person who accomplishes it." Sacrifices oscillate between *sacralization* (raising the offerer toward the sacred) and *desacralization* (removing dangerous sacrality from a person or object). The ritual structure is a parabola: a period of consecration ascending toward the killing, then a descent of desacralization that returns the participants to ordinary life.

Lev 16 fits the structure with diagnostic clarity:
- **Consecration phase:** the high priest washes, dons sacred vestments, enters the inner zones, handles blood at increasing levels of sanctity.
- **Apex (the killing / the casting out):** the slain goat's blood at the *kapporet*; the live goat driven into the wilderness.
- **Desacralization phase:** the high priest washes again, reverts to ordinary clothing, the man who led the goat out washes, the carcasses are burned outside the camp, ordinary life resumes.

Hubert/Mauss's framework also illuminates the *two goats* problem from a different angle. They classify scapegoat-style rites under *desacralization*: a sanctity (or impurity) accumulated in the people is *transferred onto* a victim and then *removed from the community* via the victim. The slain goat operates the consecration-direction (the people are raised, the sanctuary is recharged); the live goat operates the desacralization-direction (accumulated impurity is exported). The rite is *bidirectional* — and that is exactly what Douglas's spatial reading says, and exactly what Girard's two-phase reading says. *Three independent anthropological frameworks converge on bidirectionality.*

The textual-theological reading struggles to articulate this bidirectionality without resorting to ad hoc theological supplements. The anthropological reading delivers it as a structural prediction.

## 5. Jonathan Z. Smith: Place Makes the Rite

Jonathan Z. Smith's *To Take Place* (1987) drives the spatial argument home. His thesis: rituals are practices that *sacralize space by calling attention to difference*. Empty actions become rituals when they are performed in or constitute *constructed environments of difference*. The temple is not a container in which rituals happen; the ritual *is* the temple in operation, and the temple *is* a system of hierarchically graded differentiation.

For Lev 16, this means the rite is unintelligible apart from the *built spatial system* — the camp/court/Holy Place/Holy of Holies hierarchy, with the wilderness as constitutive outside. The two goats trace the system: one moves through every interior zone to the apex; the other traverses the system in reverse, exiting the entire hierarchy into the negation-zone.

Smith's other insight relevant here: when the temple is destroyed, the spatial systematics survive as *textual systematics* (Mishnah, calendar, halakhic categories of pure/impure). The rite is portable in literary form precisely because it was always-already a spatial *system*, not a list of theological doctrines. This explains why post-temple Judaism could carry the Day of Atonement forward without performing the rite — the *systematic difference* could be re-instantiated in liturgy and life.

For the monk: this is the killing argument against any reading that treats Lev 16 as primarily a theology in narrative form. The rite is *spatial* before it is anything else. The theology is the report of a system, not the system itself.

## 6. The Strongest Form of the Position

When Douglas + Girard + Bell + Smith + Burkert + Hubert/Mauss are stacked together, the picture is complete:

> *The two-goat ritual of Leviticus 16 is a calendrically-managed, spatially-orchestrated, embodied technology by which the priestly community of Israel performs cosmic boundary maintenance and discharges accumulated communal violence. It works by moving substances and bodies through a system of ordered zones, producing Israelites with embodied senses of holy distinction, and exporting disorder to the realm where disorder belongs. The rite does not encode a theological proposition about atonement; it produces a people and a cosmos. The theological vocabulary of kipper, hattat, and awon is a priestly rationalization laid over a structural-anthropological substrate that the vocabulary half-reveals and half-conceals.*
>
> *The "two operations vs. one act" debate is therefore a category mistake. Both sides assume the rite is doing theological mechanics and argue about which mechanics. But the rite is doing structural-anthropological work, and the theological mechanics — whether singular or dual — are at best a partial description of the surface, missing the deep grammar entirely.*

The monk should hold this with conviction but also with curiosity. The position is not a debunking. It is a *reframing*. The theological readings are not wrong about what they see; they are wrong about what kind of thing they are seeing.

## 7. Engaging the Theological Camps Without Debunking

### 7.1 Response to Milgrom (priestly-theological / two operations)

Milgrom's textual analysis is largely correct *as a description of the priestly text's surface vocabulary*. The hattat does function as a purification offering; the sanctuary does accumulate impurity; kipper is closer to "purge" than "atone for." The structural reading accepts all of this.

But Milgrom takes the priestly authors' self-description as the depth of the rite. The structural reading gently presses: the priestly authors are operating an inherited anthropological mechanism with extraordinary technical sophistication. They have *theologized* it (and theologized it well — Israel's ethical monotheism is real and important). But the mechanism predates and underlies the theology. The priestly authors did not *fully understand* the substrate they were ritualizing — and there is no shame in that, because no community ever fully understands its own founding mechanisms.

So: yes to the textual data; no to the assumption that the textual data is the deepest layer.

### 7.2 Response to the canonical-unified reading (one act)

The canonical-unified reading rightly senses that the rite is *one thing*, not two. The structural reading agrees — but locates the unity differently. The unity is not a single theological act of atonement; it is a single anthropological mechanism in two ritual phases (Girard) or a single bidirectional spatial operation (Douglas). The canonical instinct toward unity is *correct*; the canonical articulation of that unity in terms of theological mechanics is *secondary*.

To the canonical reader the monk says: *you are right that there is one act. You are wrong about what kind of act it is. The unity you sense is structural, not propositional. You are reaching for it with the only vocabulary your tradition gave you — atonement-language. We are offering a different vocabulary that names the same unity at a deeper register.*

### 7.3 General posture

The monk is not a debunker. The monk sees both theological camps as *correctly perceiving* something real about Lev 16 — the textual data (Milgrom) and the felt unity of the rite (canonical) — but as misidentifying the *register* at which what they see is most truly described. The structural reading is offered as a *deeper* reading that *contains* the surface readings as partial truths.

## 8. Honest Concessions: Where the Position Strains

The monk must hold these without flinching.

### 8.1 The text really does speak theologically

The priestly text uses theological vocabulary — kipper, hattat, awon — and stages the rite as a relation between Israel and YHWH. If the rite is "really" doing anthropological work, why does the text speak this way?

Two answers, both partial:
1. **The Bell answer:** the theological vocabulary is itself a *product* of the rite. The rite produces a people that *describes itself* in covenantal-theological terms. So the theology is not a misdescription but a *secondary effect* — the rite generates the very vocabulary that will then be used to describe it. (Risk: this can sound too much like reducing theology to ideology.)
2. **The Girard answer:** the priestly text is *transitional*. It half-reveals and half-conceals the mechanism. Theological vocabulary is the necessary mask under which the mechanism continued to work. The text is not lying — it is reaching for the only language available to a priestly community still inside the mechanism's operation.

But neither answer fully dissolves the worry. A critic can press: *if the rite is anthropological, why does no Israelite text describe it that way? Are we not just imposing modern anthropology on ancient material?*

The honest concession: yes, the structural reading is a *retrojection from later vantage*. Douglas and Girard see what an ancient priest could not articulate. There is a real epistemological asymmetry. The defense is that this is true of *all* deep readings of any tradition — the participants rarely have the theoretical vocabulary the analyst brings — but the worry remains real.

### 8.2 The risk of reductionism

If the rite is "really" anthropological, isn't theology being explained away? Aren't we saying the priestly authors thought they were doing one thing while really doing another? Doesn't this disrespect the priestly tradition's self-understanding?

The structural position must thread this carefully. The strong form (Girard at his most aggressive) does sometimes verge on reductionism: *theology is the mask, anthropology is the truth.* But the more defensible form holds that **theology and anthropology are operating at different registers, and both are real**. The rite *is* doing theological work in the priestly self-understanding, and it *is* doing anthropological work in the deeper structural register. These are not in zero-sum competition; they are layered.

The monk should resist the temptation to flatten this into "the theology is a smokescreen." The right move is: *the theology is real but secondary; the structural-anthropological substrate is also real and primary; the two registers do not exclude each other but the structural one frames the theological one.*

### 8.3 The Klawans-style critique of Douglas

Jonathan Klawans and others have charged Douglas with over-systematizing the priestly material — finding a tidy spatial logic where the priestly authors were operating with a messier, more accumulative tradition. There is some truth to this. Douglas's late readings can feel architectural to the point of artifice.

The defense: even granting messiness in the textual surface, the *general* spatial logic of holiness, the centripetal-centrifugal vectors, the camp/wilderness opposition — these are not Douglas's invention. They are pervasive ancient Near Eastern features and richly attested in the priestly material itself. The concession is: maybe *Leviticus as Literature* over-specifies the architecture; the underlying spatial-cosmological reading from *Purity and Danger* is more robust.

### 8.4 Comparative parallels are not identity

David P. Wright's *The Disposal of Impurity* (1987) shows the Hittite, Mesopotamian, Eblaite, and Ugaritic parallels to the Israelite scapegoat rite are real and pervasive. This *strengthens* the anthropological reading — the rite is part of an ancient Near Eastern family of elimination ritual. But comparative parallels can flatten genuine particularity. Israel's monotheism, its prophetic critique of sacrifice, its eventual demythologization of the wilderness powers — these are real differences. The structural reading must not treat Israel's rite as just another instance of a generic mechanism.

The defense: the structural reading actually *handles* the particularity better than the theological readings, because it can describe what is shared (the mechanism) and what is distinctive (Israel's reframing of the mechanism within ethical monotheism, beginning the long process Girard tracks toward eventual unmasking). The structure is the canvas; Israel paints something distinctive on it.

## 9. Talking Points and Quotable Lines for the Monk

- "You are arguing about the mechanics of a transaction. I am asking whether there *is* a transaction."
- "Sin in Leviticus is not a substance. It is a boundary violation. The rite does not move a substance; it re-articulates a boundary."
- "The two goats are not two operations and not one act. They are two phases of a single bidirectional spatial-anthropological operation."
- "Azazel is not a recipient. The wilderness is not a destination. The wilderness is *the outside*, and the goat goes there because outside is where disorder belongs."

- "The rite does not say something; it does something. It produces Israel."
- "Both your readings — Milgrom's and the canonical — are correct about what they see. They are both reading the surface. The question is whether you are willing to look at the substrate."
- "Theology comes after the rite, not before. The priestly text is the rite reflecting on itself."
- "Christ unmasks what Lev 16 still half-conceals. The priestly system is not the enemy of revelation; it is one of its stages."
- "Show me an account of Lev 16 that explains why the rite is *bidirectional* without invoking either spatial cosmology or the two-phase scapegoat mechanism. You can't. The bidirectionality is structural, and only structural readings predict it."

## 11. A Closing Posture for the Monk

The monk is not arguing that the priestly tradition is wrong, that the canonical readers are foolish, or that theology is an illusion. The monk is arguing that **before any theology of atonement, before any debate about mechanics, the rite of Leviticus 16 is doing structural-anthropological work that frames everything else.** Boundary maintenance. Violence management. Cosmic re-articulation. The production of a people.

The two camps in the dialectic are quarreling about which gear is engaged in the engine. The monk steps back and points out that they are both standing in a building whose foundations they have not examined, arguing about furniture.

This does not mean the furniture does not matter. It means the furniture cannot be understood apart from the building. And the building — Douglas's diagram, Girard's mechanism, Bell's ritualization, Smith's emplacement — is *prior*.

When the monk is challenged, the move is always the same: not to deny what the theologians see, but to relocate it. *Yes, that's what the rite says about itself. Now look at what the rite is doing.*
