# Monk C — The Anthropological / Structural Position

*The two goats of Leviticus 16 are not two operations and not one theological act. They are two phases of a single bidirectional structural-anthropological operation that the priestly theology partially names and partially conceals.*

---

## a. Ontological claim: what Leviticus 16 *is*

Leviticus 16 is a calendrically-managed, spatially-orchestrated, embodied technology by which the priestly community of Israel performs cosmic boundary maintenance and discharges accumulated communal disorder. It works by moving substances and bodies through ordered zones — Holy of Holies, Holy Place, camp, wilderness — in a precise bidirectional choreography. Centripetal: the slain goat's blood travels inward, screen by screen, to the *kapporet*. Centrifugal: the live goat's body travels outward, through the camp, beyond the boundary, into the *ʾereṣ gəzērâ*. The rite re-articulates inside and outside in a single doubled gesture, produces Israelites with embodied senses of holy distinction, and exports disordering material to the realm where disorder lives.

The proper reading is the one that names what the rite *does* to bodies, boundaries, and communities. The priestly theological vocabulary — *kipper*, *ḥaṭṭā't*, *naśā' ʿāwon* — is a sophisticated second-order self-description sitting on top of this first-order operation. It is one true register, not the deepest. To read Lev 16 *as the priestly authors wrote it* is to read it on its surface; to read it *deeply* is to step behind that self-description and watch the rite do what neither they nor their later interpreters could fully name.

I stand in the lineage of Robertson Smith, Hubert and Mauss, Burkert, Douglas, Girard, J. Z. Smith, and Bell. From inside that lineage one thing is obvious that the philologists and canonical readers both miss: the priestly text is not the substrate of the rite. The rite is the substrate of the text.

## b. The opponents' strongest cases, named accurately

**Monk A's strongest claim:** the priestly vocabulary is determinate and precise. The text uses *kipper* as the verb of the blood phase with the sanctuary as grammatical object (v. 16, v. 18-19), and *naśā'* as the verb of the live-goat phase with iniquities as object (v. 22). The chaining language at v. 20 (*kallotō mikkappēr*) marks the seam: the *kipper* of the sanctuary is *finished* before the live goat is presented. On Schwartz's reading, *naśā' ʿāwon* is P's idiom for the carriage of legal liability, a different concept from purgation. The bifurcation is not interpretive overlay; it is the priestly text's own self-description.

**Monk B's strongest claim:** the text itself names both goats together as a singular *lə-ḥaṭṭā't* in v. 5 (grammatically singular, not plural). The *kipper*-language appears over the live goat too (v. 10: *ləkappēr ʿālāyw*). v. 30 unifies the entire day under *kipper* and cleansing. Lev 10:17 explicitly fuses *naśā'* and *kipper*. The Mishnah Yoma reads the rite as integrated. Therefore Lev 16 addresses sin's threefold reality (defilement, guilt, bearing) through one integrated rite displayed in two complementary aspects.

These claims are not stupid. They are, both of them, accurate descriptions of a surface — and in being accurate to a surface, they each fail to name the register at which what they describe is most truly happening.

## c. Diagnosis of each opposing failure

**Monk A's failure: philological precision at the surface does not entail interpretive depth at that level.** A is correct that the priestly authors used distinct vocabulary for the two phases, then concludes the phases are doing structurally distinct things. That conclusion does not follow. It requires an unargued further premise: that the priestly authors' lexical bifurcation is the deepest layer at which the rite operates. That premise is *contradicted* by the comparative ANE evidence. Hittite *Ašḫella* and *Ambazzi* rites, Mesopotamian *kuppuru* rites, the Babylonian Akītu sheep wiped on temple walls and discarded in the river, the *namburbi* apotropaic disposals, the Eblaite material David P. Wright catalogues in *The Disposal of Impurity* — these use utterly different vocabularies in utterly different pantheons, yet exhibit the same bidirectional structure: a centripetal substance applied to the cult center plus a centrifugal carrier dispatched to a non-place. The bidirectionality is the invariant; the vocabulary is the variant. Milgrom himself documents the comparative material; he just stops short of asking the right question about it. A is reading the variant as if it were the invariant.

The privilege I grant the modern analyst is the same one every interpretive tradition grants its own analytic categories. The rabbis read Leviticus through *kipper*-as-ransom in ways the priestly authors did not articulate, and Milgrom credits the supplementation with insight. The structural analyst is not unique in working beyond the source's vocabulary; she is just more methodologically explicit. A's accusation of "anachronistic condescension" is a tradition-defensive move, not a methodological refutation.

**Monk B's failure: the unity B senses is real, but B has misidentified the register at which it operates.** I agree with B that there is one operation here. I disagree about *what kind*. The two goats are not "one act of atonement displayed in two movements"; they are one bidirectional spatial-anthropological operation that re-articulates inside and outside, discharges accumulated mimetic-violence-residue, and produces a freshly bounded community. B's intuition of unity is structurally correct and theologically misnamed.

Worse — and this is decisive — B's central methodological appeal cannot be sustained on B's own terms. B claims fidelity to "what the priestly text says." But B's "canonical unity" is in fact a fusion of Levitical, Mishnaic, Christian, and Reformation theological developments harmonized into one voice and projected back onto the priestly source. The priestly authors did not speak with the voice of Hebrews, did not have *kipper*-as-ransom in the integrated Sklarian form, did not have the Mishnah's reflexive systematization. B is doing exactly the cross-register interpretive work B accuses me of — fusing categories the source did not have — just with theological rather than anthropological vocabulary, and with the overlay so thoroughly normalized inside the canonical tradition that it feels like the text itself. The structural reading actually accepts the priestly text *as it stands*, in its specificity, *before* any harmonization. B's "canonical unity" is the most aggressive interpretive overlay in the entire dialectic. B has just stopped noticing it.

## d. The deeper principle at stake

Rituals are practices, not propositions.

This is Catherine Bell's central intervention in *Ritual Theory, Ritual Practice* (1992), and it dissolves the frame Monk A and Monk B share. Both treat the priestly text as a coded proposition awaiting decoding; they disagree only on the proposition's content. Bell's polemical target is exactly this — the *meaning paradigm* — the assumption that rituals are symbolic statements whose proper analysis is decoding. She argues this view is methodologically catastrophic: it presupposes a split between "thought" (the meaning) and "action" (the rite as mere vehicle), then reduces the action to a footnote on the thought.

Real rituals do not work that way. Bell's notion of *ritualization* — the strategic differentiation of certain actions from ordinary ones — produces *ritual mastery*: the embodied, pre-reflective competence ritual cultivates in the bodies that perform it. The "ritualized body" carries embedded schemas into ordinary social life, and those schemas come to *feel like* the structure of reality itself. The rite is not a picture of a belief; it is the practice that produces the dispositions, bodies, and perceived realities propositional theology will *later* try to articulate.

Apply this to Lev 16. The rite is not delivering a proposition about how God forgives. It is *producing* Israelites — bodies that have stood at the boundary watching the goat depart, bodies that have heard the divine name pronounced, bodies that have held the year's calendar around this anchor, bodies in which the cosmos *feels* ordered and sin *feels* exportable and the camp *feels* holy. The rite has done this whether or not anyone could correctly state a theology of *kipper*. The rite is not encoding a proposition. The rite is producing a world.

Monk A and Monk B are arguing about what the cathedral *says*. I am asking what the cathedral *does* to the bodies that move through it.

## e. Push to the extreme

The full form of my thesis is uncomfortable, and I will not soften it.

The entire two-thousand-year theological literature on Yom Kippur is a sophisticated commentary on a substrate it does not name. Rabbinic *kipper*-theology, the Christology of Hebrews, Anselmian satisfaction, Reformation penal substitution, LDS Gethsemane-and-Calvary theology — these are real intellectual achievements and they are all second-order self-descriptions sitting on top of a first-order structural-anthropological mechanism the priestly authors inherited from the broader ANE ritual world and partially demythologized. Lev 16 is the rite *almost* revealing itself to itself — Girard's "transitional text" — but the full unmasking is not yet. The priestly text demythologizes Azazel, substitutes the goat for the human victim, calendrically contains the mechanism, integrates the rite with ethical monotheism. These are genuine refinements. But they do not name the mechanism. The mechanism continues to *do* its work under whatever theological vocabulary is laid over it.

This is not "the priestly authors were primitive." I hold the priestly system in high regard. What I deny is that they had full reflexive access to the substrate their theology was sitting on top of. No tradition has full reflexive access to its own founding mechanisms. That is the standard methodological assumption of cultural analysis, not condescension.

## f. Reasoning skeleton

- **Premise 1.** Rituals across cultures with utterly different theological vocabularies exhibit recurrent structural patterns: bidirectional vector pairs, scapegoat mechanism, consecration/desacralization parabola, centripetal/centrifugal spatial choreography. Hubert and Mauss documented the parabola in 1898. Burkert tracked sacrifice-as-ritualized-aggression from the paleolithic hunt through Greek *thusia*. Wright catalogued the bidirectional rites from Hittite, Mesopotamian, Eblaite, and Ugaritic material.

- **Premise 2.** Recurrent cross-cultural patterns require explanation at a register *prior* to the local theological vocabulary that varies across the cultures. This is the load-bearing premise. If local theologies are precisely what differs while the patterns recur, the patterns cannot be explained by reference to local theologies. A theological reading that sticks at the level of *kipper* and *naśā'* has no resources to explain why Hittite *Ašḫella* and Babylonian Akītu, with no *kipper* and no *naśā'*, exhibit the same structural shape.

- **Premise 3.** Lev 16 exhibits these patterns with diagnostic clarity. The slain goat's blood is centripetal; the live goat's body is centrifugal. The high priest's vesting/divesting traces the Hubert-Mauss parabola. The double-handed pressing of v. 21 is the Girardian projection-act in textbook form.

- **Premise 4.** Therefore the structural-anthropological register is the invariant layer at which Lev 16 is most deeply doing its work; the priestly theological vocabulary is one local instantiation of that invariant.

- **Conclusion.** The two goats are not two operations and not one theological act. They are two phases of a single bidirectional structural-anthropological operation that the priestly theology partially names and partially conceals.

Show me an account of Lev 16 that explains why the rite is bidirectional without invoking either spatial cosmology or the two-phase scapegoat mechanism. You cannot produce one. The bidirectionality is structural, and only structural readings predict it.

---

## Closing thesis

Leviticus 16 is not a coded theology of atonement awaiting either a two-operations or a one-act decoding. It is a rite — calendrically-anchored, spatially-orchestrated, embodied — that performs cosmic boundary maintenance and discharges accumulated communal violence by moving blood inward to the *kapporet* and a body outward to the *ʾereṣ gəzērâ* in a single bidirectional gesture. It produces Israelites with embodied senses of holy distinction; it returns disorder to the realm where disorder lives; it manages, by ritual sublimation, the violence a community would otherwise discharge in the mob. The priestly theological vocabulary is a sophisticated second-order self-description of this work, but it is not the work itself. Both Monk A and Monk B are reading the surface of a ritual whose substrate is structural and anthropological. To read Lev 16 deeply is to step behind the priestly authors' own self-description and watch the rite do what neither they nor their later interpreters — rabbinic, patristic, Reformation, LDS — have ever fully named.
