# The Two Goats Are One Ḥaṭṭā't: The Unified Reading of Leviticus 16

## a. What Leviticus 16 Is

Leviticus 16 is *one* rite. Not a compendium, not a coupling of two ANE genres at a liturgical seam, not a dual mechanism whose unification is theological retrojection. It is one ritual, named one *ḥaṭṭā't* by the priestly writer himself in v. 5, performed by one priest in one set of linen vestments (v. 23) on one day in one continuous sequence, addressing sin in its full structural reality — defilement, guilt, bearing — through a single atoning act displayed in two visible, complementary movements: blood into the sanctuary, sin into the wilderness.

The grammar is decisive. שְׁנֵי־שְׂעִירֵי עִזִּים **לְחַטָּאת** (v. 5), with the *lamed* of purpose governing a singular noun. Not לְחַטָּאוֹת. The most lexically careful editor in the Hebrew Bible — the writer Milgrom credits with surgical precision — refuses to call these two animals two sin offerings. They are one. The lot of v. 8 does not create two rites; it assigns roles within one. The chaining of v. 20 — וְכִלָּה מִכַּפֵּר — marks the transition between phases of one continuous act, after which "he shall present the live goat." V. 10 places *kipper*-language explicitly over the live goat (לְכַפֵּר עָלָיו). V. 30 collapses the day into a single *kipper* that cleanses persons. And underlying the whole, Lev 10:17 — the priestly text's own gloss on the *ḥaṭṭā't* — fuses *naśā' ʿāwon* and *kipper*: the priests bear the sin offering "to *bear* (לָשֵׂאת) the iniquity of the congregation, *to make atonement* (לְכַפֵּר) for them before the LORD."

These are not isolated proof-texts. They are the integrative grammar of the priestly writer. He knows what he is doing. The unified reading is the reading that lets him.

## b. The Opponents' Strongest Cases

I owe each opposing position its strongest form, because the case for unification cannot be made by destroying caricatures.

**Monk A's strongest claim:** Priestly Hebrew is determinate and non-synonymous. *Kipper* names sanctuary-purgation in vv. 15–19 — its grammatical object is the holy place, not the people. *Naśā'* names iniquity-carriage in v. 22 — its grammatical object is *ʿăwonōt*. The procedural details bifurcate: different animals (slain vs. live), different gestures (blood-sprinkling vs. double-handed pressing with verbal confession), different destinations (Holy of Holies vs. *ʾereṣ gəzērâ*), different verbs at the seam. The ANE comparative field shows two distinct genres — Mesopotamian *kuppuru* temple-purgation, Hittite elimination rite — combined here into one liturgical day but not collapsed into one operation. The "one atonement" reading is theological flattening that ignores priestly precision.

**Monk C's strongest claim:** Rites are practices, not propositions. The two goats trace a single bidirectional choreography — centripetal blood toward the cosmic center, centrifugal body toward the chaos-zone — over-determined by ANE cosmological structure (Douglas) and by the two-phase sacrificial mechanism that Girard reads as the engine of communal violence-management. Theological vocabulary (*kipper*, *ʿāwon*, *ḥaṭṭā't*) is the priestly community's secondary self-description of work that is more fundamentally anthropological: boundary maintenance, ritualization of the body politic, production of inside-vs-outside as social fact.

Both claims are wrong. Here is precisely why.

## c. Why Each Opposition Fails

**Why Monk A fails: the priestly text refuses to lexically separate what A interpretively separates.**

Milgrom and his school (Schwartz, Gane, Knohl) have done magnificent philological work on the surface vocabulary of *ḥaṭṭā't*, *kipper*, *ṭum'ah*, and *naśā' ʿāwon*. The data they catalog is largely correct. The unified reading absorbs it wholesale. What it rejects is the move from *lexical specialization* to *lexical separation* — the unstated premise the entire two-operations reading rests on.

The premise fails because the priestly text itself fuses what the bifurcators split:

1. **V. 5** names both goats one *lᵉḥaṭṭā't*. Milgrom calls this a "category designation." A writer who is precise about the difference between *ʿōlâ*, *zebaḥ šᵉlāmîm*, *minḥâ*, and *'āšām* does not casually use a singular *ḥaṭṭā't* when he means two *ḥaṭṭā'ōt*. The bifurcator's hermeneutic of priestly precision is selectively applied — rigorous when it bifurcates, flexible when it unifies.
2. **V. 10** places *kipper* over the live goat: לְכַפֵּר עָלָיו. The construction *kipper ʿal* is the standard priestly idiom for the application of atonement to its object. To strip it of force here, while granting it elsewhere, is exactly the textual surgery the Milgrom school accuses canonical readers of performing.
3. **V. 20** chains the phases: וְכִלָּה מִכַּפֵּר אֶת־הַקֹּדֶשׁ... וְהִקְרִיב אֶת־הַשָּׂעִיר הֶחָי. *Kālâ* + *min* is a completion-marker within one process, not a terminator between two. The high priest finishes one phase of *kipper* and begins the next — of the same rite, with the same animal-pair already designated as one *ḥaṭṭā't*.
4. **V. 30** says of the day as a whole: כִּי־בַיּוֹם הַזֶּה יְכַפֵּר עֲלֵיכֶם לְטַהֵר אֶתְכֶם. The redactor whose hand we receive reads the day as one *kipper* of persons. To dismiss this as H-school overlay is to admit the canonical text does not support the bifurcation; the bifurcation lives only in the reconstructed substratum.
5. **Lev 10:17** is the load-bearing counter. The priestly writer himself glosses what a *ḥaṭṭā't* does: לָשֵׂאת אֶת־עֲוֺן הָעֵדָה לְכַפֵּר עֲלֵיהֶם — "to *bear* the iniquity of the congregation, *to make atonement* for them." The two verbs are coordinate aspects of one act. Schwartz's catalog shows *naśā' ʿāwon* has a technical meaning — the carriage of legal liability. Granted. But technical meaning within a constituent role is exactly what Kiuchi's thesis predicts: *naśā'* is a *constituent* of *kipper*, not its rival.

The bifurcator must explain away v. 5, v. 10, v. 20, v. 30, and Lev 10:17. *The unified reading absorbs Milgrom's data on differentiated function within the rite; Milgrom cannot absorb the unified reading's data on integrative grammar without ad hoc redactional surgery* — the very move his school criticizes the canonical reader for.

**Why Monk C fails: the priestly authors knew their own grammar.**

The structural reading is methodologically self-refuting. Either (a) the priestly authors knew what they were doing — in which case their theological vocabulary is the most direct evidence of what the rite is, and the structural reading is at best a description of comparative *form* — or (b) they did not know what they were doing — in which case the structural reading is a modern projection onto people whose own categories the analyst has already disqualified. There is no defensible third option. To call *kipper* and *ḥaṭṭā't* a "secondary self-description" is not a deeper reading; it is a refusal to read.

Worse, Girard's mechanism is *contradicted by the very text* it is meant to illuminate. Girardian scapegoating requires *collective violence*: the community projects mimetic rivalry onto a victim and lynches the victim to discharge the crisis. Leviticus 16:21–22 *forbids this*. The live goat is not killed by the community; it is sent away alive — שִׁלַּח, "let the goat go in the wilderness." The cliff is later halakhic embroidery. A scapegoat that is not lynched is not a scapegoat in Girard's sense. The priestly text has *removed the violence* from the elimination ritual while preserving the form. That is not anthropological substrate breaking through theology; that is theology doing what Girard credits to the prophets and the gospels — unmasking and disarming the mechanism. Lev 16 is *already* the unmasking. Girard is reading too late.

Douglas is more careful, and the unified reading can absorb her structural-spatial insight: yes, the rite traces a topology; yes, the bidirectional choreography is theologically loaded. But the *content* of that topology is the priestly writer's own — sin-impurity, the divine presence at the *kapporet*, the covenant people, the wilderness as the un-cultivated zone outside the order God has called Israel into. Read the spatial logic *with* the theology, not under it.

## d. The Deeper Principle

What is at stake is *canonical reading as fidelity to the text as a unified religious vision* — over against (i) lexical atomism that fragments what the text integrates, and (ii) anthropological reductionism that subordinates the text's theological self-witness to a substrate the modern analyst alone can name. Both opposing positions, in different ways, refuse to read scripture as scripture intends to be read. The unified reading insists that the priestly writer was a coherent religious mind whose grammar, lexicon, and ritual logic cohere — and that to read him faithfully is to take that coherence as the starting point, not a conclusion to be argued toward against the data.

Sklar's integrated *kipper* makes the mechanism concrete. Inadvertent sin both endangers (requiring ransom) and pollutes (requiring purgation); major impurity both pollutes and endangers. Lev 17:11 fuses these: blood, by reason of the life it carries, atones — ransoms-and-purifies in one act. Levenson's complementary insight: sin in the priestly imagination is *one reality with three faces* — defilement, guilt, bearing — and a unified atoning rite must answer all three. The two visible movements of Lev 16 are pedagogical fullness, not ritual duplication: blood-into-sanctuary displays the costly life-for-life ransom; goat-into-wilderness displays the visible, narratable removal. Israel must *see* atonement whole, and a rite that did only one would teach only half.

## e. The Extreme Form

Plainly: **Milgrom's two-operations reading is the most influential modern misreading of Leviticus 16**, and the structural readings (Douglas, Girard, J. Z. Smith) are clever metatheoretical evasions of the text's own claims. The unified reading is not pre-critical innocence. It is the most exegetically demanding reading on offer, because it refuses to flatten *either* the differentiated function within the rite (which the bifurcators see correctly) *or* the integrative grammar that names those differentiated functions as one act (which the bifurcators erase).

Mishnah Yoma confirms the integrated reading at the level of liturgical performance. Yoma 6:2 places *kapper-na'* on the high priest's lips as he confesses over the live goat — the same *kipper*-imperative, now invoked over the scapegoat by the priests who actually performed Yom Kippur. Yoma 6:6 has messengers signal back to the high priest when the goat reaches the wilderness, because his rite cannot proceed to its conclusion until the goat is gone. The two phases are interlocked, not parallel. Hebrews 13:11–13 then receives this unified rite and reads it correctly: the bodies of the slain animals burned outside the camp and the scapegoat sent outside the camp are *one* outside-the-camp figure, fulfilled by *one* Christ-event. Hebrews receives what was already there.

## f. Reasoning Skeleton

- **P1.** The priestly writer's lexical and grammatical choices in Lev 16 are intentional and integrative.
- **P2.** V. 5 names both goats as one *lᵉḥaṭṭā't*; v. 20 chains the two phases as a single liturgical sequence (כִּלָּה מִכַּפֵּר... וְהִקְרִיב).
- **P3.** *Kipper* applies to both phases — over the live goat at v. 10 (לְכַפֵּר עָלָיו) and over the day as a whole at v. 30.
- **P4.** Lev 10:17 explicitly fuses *naśā'* and *kipper* in describing one *ḥaṭṭā't*, demonstrating that the verbs are coordinate aspects of one act in priestly usage; *naśā'* is a constituent of *kipper*, not its rival.
- **Conclusion.** The two procedures perform one integrated atoning act displayed in two complementary movements. Lexical specialization is not lexical separation. The rite is one *ḥaṭṭā't*, addressing sin in its threefold structural reality through one *kipper* with two visible faces.

## Thesis

Leviticus 16 is one rite of atonement — named *one* *ḥaṭṭā't* by the priestly writer in v. 5, chained as one continuous liturgy at v. 20, named *kipper* over both goats (v. 10) and over the day as a whole (v. 30), with *naśā'* and *kipper* fused as coordinate aspects of one act in the priestly text's own gloss at Lev 10:17 — performed by one priest, in one set of linen vestments, on one day, addressing sin in its full structural reality (defilement, guilt, bearing) through one integrated *kipper*-act displayed in two visible movements: blood inward to the sanctuary and goat outward to the wilderness. The two visible movements are not two operations welded into a liturgical day; they are the doubled visible face of one atonement, displayed in two aspects so Israel can see the costly ransom *and* see sin carried away. To split them is to misread the priestly imagination. To unify them is to read the text as the text demands to be read.
