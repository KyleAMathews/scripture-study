## FRAMING CORRECTIONS

**Your argument is NOT "the priestly text is self-evidently theological and doesn't require historical-critical reading."** All three positions take historical and literary context seriously. The real difference is whether the priestly text's *integrative grammar and ritual logic* are taken as authoritative for what the rite is, or whether they are subordinated to either lexical fragmentation (Monk A) or anthropological substrate (Monk C).

**Your argument is NOT "Hebrews settles it."** Hebrews is decisive in a *canonical* reading — but you must show first that the unification is *grammatically anchored in Leviticus itself* (v. 5, v. 10, v. 20, v. 30) and *liturgically anchored in Mishnah Yoma* before invoking Hebrews. Hebrews receives what was already there.

**Against Monk A specifically:** A will press the lexical distinction between *kipper* (vv. 15-19) and *naśā'* (v. 22), the absence of slaughter for the live goat, the unique double-handed pressing, and the ANE generic distinction between temple-*kuppuru* and elimination rites. Your specific rebuttal: lexical specialization is NOT lexical separation. *Lev 10:17* is decisive — it explicitly fuses *naśā' ʿāwon* and *kipper* in describing the *same* ḥaṭṭā't ("to bear the iniquity of the congregation, to make atonement for them"). The two verbs are coordinate aspects of one act, not labels for two acts. Kiuchi's argument: *naśā'* is a *constituent* of *kipper*, not its rival. Furthermore, A cannot account for the priestly text's own integrative moves (v. 5, v. 10, v. 20-chain) without ad hoc redactional surgery — which is precisely the move A's school criticizes the canonical reader for.

**Against Monk C specifically:** C will argue that the rite is doing structural-anthropological work and that theological vocabulary is a "secondary self-description." Your specific rebuttal: this is methodologically self-refuting. Either (a) the priestly authors knew what they were doing — in which case their theological vocabulary is the most direct evidence of what the rite is, and the structural reading is at best a description of comparative form — or (b) they did not know what they were doing — in which case the structural reading is a modern projection onto people whose own categories the analyst dismisses. There is no defensible third option. Furthermore: Girard's mechanism *requires collective violence*; Lev 16 *forbids it* (the goat is not killed in the biblical text — the cliff is later halakha). The canonical answer to Girard is: the priestly text is *already* doing the unmasking Girard credits to the prophets and the gospels — it has *removed the violence* from the scapegoat ritual while preserving the form. That is theological revolution, not anthropological substrate.
