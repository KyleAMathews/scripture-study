# The Historical Development of "The Atonement" as a Unified Concept

A research report tracing how diverse biblical, theological, and ritual operations came to be bundled under a single English-language label.

---

## 1. Etymology and Origin of the English Word

The word "atonement" is almost the only theological term of English origin. It does not derive from Hebrew, Greek, or Latin. It was built from the Middle English adverbial phrase *atonen* (c. 1300), meaning "in accord," literally "at one." The noun form "atonement" emerged in the 1510s, meaning "condition of being at one with others." Its theological sense -- reconciliation of man with God -- dates to the 1520s.

**William Tyndale** is the pivotal figure. In his 1526 New Testament, he deployed "atonement" at Romans 5:11 to translate the Greek *katallagē*: "We also joy in God by the means of our Lord Jesus Christ, by whom we have received the atonement." He also used it at 2 Corinthians 5:18. In his 1530 Pentateuch, he applied the same word to the Hebrew *kaphar*/*kippur* in the Levitical passages.

The critical point is that Tyndale used a single English word to translate terms from two different languages that meant different things:

- **Hebrew *kaphar***: The verb most often rendered "atonement" in the Old Testament. Scholarly debate over its root meaning has produced three candidates: (1) "to cover," from a cognate appearing in Genesis 6:14 where Noah covers the ark with pitch; (2) "to ransom," connected to the noun *kopher* (ransom price); and (3) "to wipe clean" or "to purge," which recent scholarship increasingly favors. The verb is never used in the simple Qal stem in Hebrew -- only in derived intensive stems -- which suggests its meaning is not simply "cover" but something more active like ritual purging or cleansing. The noun *kippur* (as in *Yom Kippur*) derives from this root.

- **Greek *katallagē***: The word Tyndale was actually translating at Romans 5:11. It means "exchange" or "reconciliation" -- a restoration of relationship between estranged parties. It carries no connotation of covering, purging, or sacrifice. It is a relational, not a ritual, term.

- **Greek *hilasmos*/*hilastērion***: Translated variously as "propitiation" or "expiation." *Hilastērion* in the Septuagint designates the mercy seat on the Ark of the Covenant -- the physical "place of propitiation" in the Day of Atonement ritual. In the New Testament (Romans 3:25, 1 John 2:2), it carries connotations of appeasing wrath or removing offense.

- **Greek *apolytrōsis***: "Redemption" -- the act of freeing someone by paying a ransom price. Paul uses it in Ephesians 1:7 and Romans 3:24. The underlying metaphor is the slave market or prisoner exchange, not the temple altar.

- **Greek *dikaioō***: "Justification" -- a forensic, legal term meaning "to declare righteous." Paul uses it in Romans 5:9.

These are not synonyms. They come from different conceptual worlds: the temple, the courtroom, the slave market, the estranged household. The English word "atonement" -- or more precisely, the theological habit of treating "the Atonement" as a single unified event -- papers over these distinctions.

As the [International Standard Bible Encyclopedia](https://www.internationalstandardbible.com/A/atonement.html) notes: "The English word 'atonement' does not correspond etymologically with any Hebrew or Greek word which it translates. Furthermore, the Greek words in both Septuagint and New Testament do not correspond exactly to the Hebrew words."

## 2. Biblical Usage: One Act or Many?

### The Old Testament Sacrificial System

The Old Testament does not treat what we call "the Atonement" as a single operation. It presents a complex *system* of distinct ritual acts, each serving a different function.

The five major types of Levitical sacrifice (*korbanot*) are:

1. **Olah** (Burnt Offering): The entire animal was consumed on the altar. It expressed complete dedication to God and made atonement for unintentional sin.
2. **Minhah** (Grain/Meal Offering): An offering of fine flour or unleavened baked goods mixed with oil. A gift of gratitude, not a blood sacrifice.
3. **Shelamim** (Peace/Well-being Offering): Divided among the altar, priests, and the offerer. Associated with communion, feasting, and relational wholeness.
4. **Hattat** (Sin Offering): Offered for unintentional sins. The blood was applied to specific locations to purify sacred space from the contamination of sin.
5. **Asham** (Guilt/Reparation Offering): A specialized sin offering for offenses that could be estimated by monetary value, requiring restitution alongside the sacrifice.

These are five distinct ritual operations with different purposes: dedication, gratitude, communion, purification, and restitution. They do not collapse into one.

**Yom Kippur (the Day of Atonement)** itself, described in Leviticus 16, involves multiple distinct sub-operations:

- Aaron offers a **bull as a sin offering** for himself and his household.
- He burns **incense** before the mercy seat so the smoke covers it, preventing his death in God's presence.
- He takes **two goats**: one is slaughtered as a sin offering for the people (its blood sprinkled on the mercy seat), and the other -- the **scapegoat** (*azazel*) -- has the sins of Israel confessed over it and is driven into the wilderness alive.
- Finally, **burnt offerings** (rams) are offered for both priest and people.

The slaughtered goat and the scapegoat perform *different operations*: one purifies sacred space through blood, the other removes sin by carrying it away. These are complementary but distinct mechanisms -- purification and elimination. As the Jewish Learning site [notes](https://www.myjewishlearning.com/article/leviticus-161-34-the-scapegoat-ritual/), the two goats are treated as "one sacrifice" but they perform different symbolic work.

### The New Testament

In the King James Version, the word "atonement" appears **77 times in the Old Testament** (43 of those in Leviticus alone) but **only once in the New Testament**: Romans 5:11. The New Testament instead uses a diverse vocabulary of distinct terms:

- **Reconciliation** (*katallagē*): Romans 5:10-11, 2 Corinthians 5:18-20. The relational restoration between God and humanity.
- **Redemption** (*apolytrōsis*, *lutroō*): Ephesians 1:7, Titus 2:14, Mark 10:45. Liberation through payment of a price -- the slave-market metaphor.
- **Propitiation** (*hilasmos*, *hilastērion*): Romans 3:25, 1 John 2:2. The turning away of God's wrath or the cleansing of offense.
- **Justification** (*dikaioō*): Romans 5:9, Romans 3:24. A legal declaration of righteousness.
- **Sanctification** (*hagiasmos*): 1 Thessalonians 4:3, Hebrews 10:10. Being made holy, set apart.
- **Victory** (*nikos*): 1 Corinthians 15:54-57. Christ's triumph over death and the powers of evil.

Paul, the New Testament's primary theologian of salvation, moves fluidly among these metaphors without reducing them to one. As N.T. Wright has argued, Paul's diverse metaphorical frameworks -- sacrifice, redemption, justification, reconciliation, victory -- are "not random, nor interchangeable" but are each grounded in distinct aspects of the scriptural story. Wright specifically warns against collapsing all Old Testament sacrifice into a single penal-substitutionary metaphor: "Sacrifice means a wide variety of different things in the O.T. There are sin offerings and guilt offerings and thank offerings and so on."

## 3. Patristic and Medieval Development

### The First Thousand Years: No Unified Theory

The early Church Fathers did **not** possess or articulate a single, unified "doctrine of the Atonement." As the [Catholic Encyclopedia](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/02055a.htm) acknowledges: "At first the central fact was made known in the Apostolic preaching, that mankind was fallen and redeemed by the blood of Christ, but it remained for the pious speculation of Fathers and theologians to enter into the meaning of this great truth."

The [Nicene Creed](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/atonement/) (325 CE) affirms that Christ came down from heaven "for us and for our salvation" and was crucified, but it notably avoids specifying *how* Christ's death saves. This interpretive space was never officially closed.

Multiple complementary frameworks emerged among the Fathers:

**Recapitulation (Irenaeus, c. 130-202)**: The earliest developed model. Irenaeus saw Christ as the "new Adam" who succeeds where Adam failed, recapitulating (summing up and redoing) every stage of human life to reverse the effects of the Fall. The goal was *theosis* -- making humanity divine. Irenaeus wrote that Christ "became what we are, that He might bring us to be even what He is Himself." This is not a legal or penal framework; it is participatory and transformative.

**Ransom Theory (Origen, c. 185-254; Gregory of Nyssa, c. 335-395)**: Christ's death was a ransom paid to the devil, who held humanity in bondage. In some versions, the devil was deceived -- God offered Christ as "bait" and the devil, by killing an innocent person, lost all rights over humanity. This was the **dominant theory for roughly a thousand years**.

**Christus Victor**: Gustaf Aulen, in his influential 1930 book *Christus Victor*, argued that the "classic" view of the early church, held from Irenaeus through Augustine, understood salvation as Christ's dramatic victory over the powers of sin, death, and the devil. Aulen insisted this was "not so much a rational systematic theory as it is a drama" -- a cosmic narrative rather than a legal equation.

**Greek Fathers and Mystical Redemption**: The Greek patristic tradition emphasized that salvation occurs through the Incarnation itself -- God becoming human so that humans might become divine. Athanasius, Gregory of Nazianzus, and later Maximus the Confessor developed this into the Eastern Orthodox doctrine of *theosis* (deification). The Orthodox tradition to this day does not treat the Atonement primarily through legal or satisfaction categories but through healing, restoration, and participation in divine life.

The [Catholic Encyclopedia](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/02055a.htm) admits plainly: "Most, if not all, of these theories had perils of their own if they were isolated and exaggerated." The various models "are not mutually exclusive, but may be combined and harmonized" and "all help to bring out different aspects of that great doctrine which cannot find adequate expression in any human theory."

### Anselm's Revolution (1098)

**Anselm of Canterbury** (1033-1109) wrote *Cur Deus Homo* ("Why God Became Man") in 1094-1098, and this work fundamentally reshaped Western atonement theology. Anselm found the ransom theory -- which had been accepted for a thousand years -- no longer communicative. His **satisfaction theory** drew on feudal concepts of honor: human sin defrauds God of the honor due to Him. Since the offense is against an infinite being, only an infinite satisfaction can restore the balance. Christ, as both God and man, freely offers his death as supererogatory obedience, generating more honor than he owed, and this surplus can be applied to humanity's debt.

Anselm's work was revolutionary because it was the **first systematic attempt to provide a rational, unified theory of atonement**. Before Anselm, there was diversity and metaphor; after Anselm, there was increasingly a felt need for a single controlling explanation. His treatise "became a cornerstone of scholastic theology, establishing the satisfaction theory as normative in major summae."

### Abelard's Counter-proposal (c. 1140)

**Peter Abelard** (1079-1142) rejected both the ransom theory and Anselm's satisfaction theory. He argued that Christ's death was not a payment to anyone -- neither to the devil nor to God's honor -- but rather a **demonstration of God's love** that transforms the hearts of sinners. This "moral influence" or "subjective" theory emphasized that the change happens in us, not in God. Abelard argued: "It is not God who needs to be reconciled to us; it is we who need to be reconciled to him."

Abelard was condemned by the Council of Sens in 1140 and eventually excommunicated, partly through the opposition of Bernard of Clairvaux. But his insight -- that different theories locate the "problem" and the "solution" in different places -- exposed the plurality within what was increasingly being called "the" doctrine of atonement.

## 4. The Reformation Split

The Reformation did not directly dispute the doctrine of the Atonement in the way it disputed justification, the sacraments, or papal authority. But it produced a new model that became dominant in Protestantism and drove Catholicism to differentiate its own position more sharply.

### Penal Substitutionary Atonement (Luther and Calvin)

Martin Luther and, more systematically, **John Calvin** developed **penal substitution**: Christ did not merely restore God's honor (Anselm's satisfaction) but bore the actual *punishment* that sinners deserved. God's justice demands that sin be punished; Christ takes that punishment in our place. As Calvin articulated it, God pours out his wrath on the Son so that the demands of justice are met and sinners can be declared righteous (justified) by faith alone.

This differs from Anselm's model in a critical way: for Anselm, Christ offers positive honor to compensate for lost honor; for Calvin, Christ absorbs negative punishment. The underlying legal logic shifts from feudal honor-debt to criminal penalty. Penal substitution became the controlling framework in Reformed Protestantism and remains the dominant understanding in much of evangelical Christianity.

### Catholic Response

The **Council of Trent** (1545-1563) formalized Catholic soteriology in response. The Sixth Session addressed justification, and the Twenty-Second Session affirmed the Mass as "a true propitiatory sacrifice." Catholic theology **rejected penal substitution** as the primary or controlling explanation. In the Catholic account, God does not pour out His wrath on the Son; rather, Christ offers a positive gift of love and obedience to the Father.

Catholicism maintained that multiple models -- Christus Victor, moral exemplar, and sacrificial satisfaction (of Anselm's type, not Calvin's) -- all capture genuine aspects of the mystery. The Catholic position preserved the pre-Reformation pluralism of models while explicitly rejecting the Protestant tendency to elevate one model (penal substitution) as the single controlling explanation.

### Eastern Orthodox Position

The Eastern Orthodox tradition largely continued the patristic trajectory and remained unaffected by the Western medieval and Reformation debates. Orthodox theology understands the Fall "not primarily in the categories of guilt and punishment but of mortality and corruption." Salvation is *theosis* -- participation in divine life -- not primarily forgiveness of a legal debt. The Orthodox approach is deliberately pluralistic: "Many different metaphors and images are used to express different aspects of this mystery." Orthodoxy "prefers other metaphors like healing, victory, and deification" over satisfaction or penal substitution.

This means that by the 17th century, Christianity had produced at least three major streams with different understandings of what "the Atonement" encompasses:
- **Protestant (especially Reformed)**: Penal substitution as the central, controlling model.
- **Catholic**: Satisfaction + multiple complementary models, with penal substitution rejected.
- **Orthodox**: Theosis/deification, Christus Victor, and healing as primary frameworks, with Western legal categories largely absent.

## 5. Latter-day Saint Theological Development

### Joseph Smith's Usage

Joseph Smith did use the word "atonement," but not with the frequency or centrality that modern Latter-day Saints might expect. In the Doctrine and Covenants, he wrote: "made perfect through Jesus the mediator of the new covenant, who wrought out this perfect atonement through the shedding of his own blood" ([D&C 76:69](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/dc-testament/dc/76.69)). The Third Article of Faith states: "We believe that through the atonement of Christ all mankind may be saved by obedience to the laws and ordinances of the Gospel."

But Joseph Smith's theological emphasis was overwhelmingly **crucicentric** -- focused on the cross and resurrection, not on "the Atonement" as a separate, unified theological concept. According to research by [John Hilton III at BYU's Religious Studies Center](https://rsc.byu.edu/how-what-you-worship/joseph-smith-gethsemane-crucifixion-jesus-christ), Joseph Smith's Doctrine and Covenants revelations contain only **one reference to Gethsemane but at least fifteen references to the Crucifixion**. His foundational doctrinal statement was: "The fundamental principles of our religion are the testimony of the Apostles and Prophets, concerning Jesus Christ, that He died, was buried, and rose again the third day, and ascended into heaven; and all other things which pertain to our religion are only appendages to it." Gethsemane is not mentioned.

The Lectures on Faith (1834-35), the earliest attempt at systematic Latter-day Saint theology, focused on the nature of faith, the character of God, and the conditions for salvation -- not on a developed atonement theology.

### The Book of Mormon

The Book of Mormon uses the words "atone," "atoneth," and "atonement" approximately **35-39 times** across about 24 verses. (The King James Bible Old Testament uses it 77 times; the KJV New Testament uses it once.) The Book of Mormon's major doctrinal treatments appear in:

- **2 Nephi 2**: Lehi's teaching on the Fall, opposition, and the necessity of a Messiah.
- **2 Nephi 9**: Jacob's discourse on the "infinite atonement," resurrection, and God's plan.
- **Mosiah 3**: King Benjamin's angelic message about Christ's suffering and blood atonement.
- **Alma 34**: Amulek on the "great and last sacrifice" that must be "infinite and eternal."
- **Alma 42**: Alma's teaching on the relationship between justice, mercy, and the atonement.

The Book of Mormon introduced the distinctive phrase "infinite atonement" (2 Nephi 9:7, Alma 34:10, 12), which has no direct biblical parallel. It also established a theological framework linking the Atonement to the Fall of Adam in a tight dialectical relationship ("Adam fell that men might be; and men are, that they might have joy" -- 2 Nephi 2:25).

Notably, the Book of Mormon's atonement passages tend to emphasize **blood, sacrifice, and death** -- not Gethsemane specifically. The orientation is broadly consistent with the crucicentric emphasis of early Christianity and of Joseph Smith's own teachings.

### Doctrine and Covenants and Pearl of Great Price

The word "atonement" appears approximately **11 times in the Doctrine and Covenants** and **3 times in the Pearl of Great Price**. Neither volume develops an extended atonement theology comparable to the Book of Mormon's major discourses.

### The Gethsemane Shift (1880s-1980s)

One of the most distinctive developments in LDS atonement theology is the gradual relocation of the primary locus of Christ's atoning work from the **cross** to the **Garden of Gethsemane**. This shift has been termed "horticentrism" (garden-centered) as opposed to the traditional Christian "crucicentrism" (cross-centered).

The timeline of this shift, as documented by [Kyle Beshears](https://www.kylebeshears.com/p/why-latter-day-saints-emphasized) and [John Hilton III](https://rsc.byu.edu/how-what-you-worship/joseph-smith-gethsemane-crucifixion-jesus-christ):

- **1830s-1880s**: Early LDS teaching resembled mainstream Protestant crucicentrism. The 1830 Articles and Covenants confess that Christ "was crucified, died, and rose again the third day."
- **1882**: **John Taylor** published *The Mediation and Atonement*, which made early statements suggesting garden-inclusive atonement.
- **1915**: **James E. Talmage** published *Jesus the Christ*, giving enduring shape to the Gethsemane emphasis. He wrote that Christ's agony in Gethsemane "caused Him to suffer such torture as to produce an extrusion of blood from every pore" and that in the garden Christ "took upon Himself the burden of the sins of mankind."
- **1947**: **Joseph Fielding Smith** declared Christ's "greatest suffering was not on the cross" but in the garden.
- **1985**: **Bruce R. McConkie** delivered what has been called "the clearest articulation of the mature horticentric model," distinguishing Gethsemane as where sin-bearing occurred while Calvary merely completed it.

A BYU survey found that **88% of students** believed "the Atonement of Christ mostly took place" in the Garden of Gethsemane, while only 12% chose the Cross at Calvary.

### The Rise of "The Atonement" in LDS Discourse

Corpus analysis of LDS General Conference talks reveals a dramatic quantitative shift. Using the [BYU Corpus of LDS General Conference Talks](https://www.lds-general-conference.org/) (10,000+ addresses, 24 million+ words from 1851 to present):

- **1942 to late 1990s**: The phrase "atonement of Jesus Christ" appeared **fewer than 50 times per million words**.
- **Late 1990s**: Usage roughly doubled to just under **100 per million words**.
- **2012**: Usage peaked at approximately **200 per million words** -- a fourfold increase from the mid-20th century baseline.

This means that "the Atonement" as a central organizing concept in LDS discourse is substantially a **late 20th-century development**. The phrase was not absent before the 1990s, but it was not the dominant theological framework it later became. The intensification correlates with several factors: the publication of influential books like Tad R. Callister's *The Infinite Atonement* (2000), a broader institutional emphasis on Christocentrism, and increasing engagement with evangelical Christian theological categories.

Terryl Givens has observed that "the first Christians had no such theology. They simply knew that in some way, Christ had taken their pains and sufferings and sins upon Himself." He argues that Latter-day Saints have been "influenced by Christian traditions that emphasize the very notions of equity and fairness repudiated by Jesus in the New Testament -- which can lead to flawed perceptions of salvation." Givens frames atonement as fundamentally about "reintegration into loving relationship -- not just vertically with God, but horizontally with those around us."

Bruce R. McConkie himself acknowledged the problem: the Atonement is "the most basic and fundamental doctrine of the gospel, and it is the least understood of all our revealed truths."

## 6. LDS Scholars on the Unity/Plurality Question

Several LDS scholars have directly addressed the problem of treating "the Atonement" as a single, self-evident concept:

**T. Benjamin Spackman**, in "The Israelite Roots of Atonement Terminology" (*BYU Studies Quarterly* 55:1, 2016), argues that Latter-day Saints use "atone," "redeem," and "save" **interchangeably**, though they had sharply distinct meanings in their Hebrew origins:

- **Atonement** (*kaphar*): "primarily priestly, having to do with ritual purity and pollution."
- **Redemption** (*ga'al*, *padah*): "primarily in terms of kinship and family law" -- the obligation of a relative to buy back a family member from slavery or debt.
- **Salvation** (*yasha*): "often found in political or martial contexts, where 'victory' or even 'success' is a more direct translation."

Spackman concludes that "current LDS usage of these English terms represents a shift (or several) from their meaning in the sources." The Israelite originals were "grounded in the concrete, this-worldly, and practical," whereas modern usage has made them "highly theological, eschatological, and heavenly." The bundling of these distinct operations under one label obscures what each originally meant.

**Blake Ostler**, in his four-volume *Exploring Mormon Thought* series and in his essay "Atonement in Mormon Thought," developed a "Compassion Theory of Atonement" that emphasizes mutual reconciliation: "The purpose of the Atonement is to overcome our alienation by creating compassion, a life shared in union where we are moved by our love for each other." The atonement "not only reconciles us to God, but also reconciles God to us." Ostler has also critiqued the contributors to *Latter-day Saint Perspectives on Atonement* (2024) for ignoring sixty years of philosophical theology on atonement and for mischaracterizing the Church Fathers' views.

**Adam S. Miller**, an LDS philosopher, has argued that "Grace is original. Grace is what comes first, and it is sin that then comes in response." Miller challenges the traditional LDS framing in which grace is understood as a transactional response to human sinfulness, accessed through Christ's atonement. He argues that this transactional approach "not only cheapens the atonement itself, but negates the primacy of grace."

**Lorin K. Hansen** published "The 'Moral' Atonement as a Mormon Interpretation" in *Dialogue: A Journal of Mormon Thought* (Vol. 27, No. 1, Spring 1994), arguing for an Abelardian moral-influence reading of atonement within LDS theology -- a direct challenge to the penal-substitutionary framework that most Latter-day Saints unconsciously adopt.

The **2024 volume** *Latter-day Saint Perspectives on Atonement*, edited by Deidre Nicole Green and Eric D. Huntsman (University of Illinois Press), represents the most comprehensive scholarly treatment to date. Contributors include Fiona Givens (who challenges satisfaction theories as wrongly sanctioning violence), Joseph M. Spencer (who argues for nonviolent atonement), and Nicholas J. Frederick (who traces the development of blood-atonement language across Book of Mormon authors). A review in *Times and Seasons* notes that the volume reframes atonement "from transactional to relational, emphasizing communal restoration alongside individual redemption."

## 7. Critical Voices on the Unity/Plurality Question

Beyond LDS scholarship, several theologians have directly critiqued the practice of bundling diverse divine operations under a single label:

**The "Multiple Metaphors" School**: A growing consensus in biblical theology holds that the New Testament itself resists reduction to a single atonement theory. As one Cambridge study on atonement methodology puts it: "given the nature of metaphor, it is unthinkable that one soteriological model could express all of the truth." The diverse range of New Testament imagery is *necessary* because no single model can capture the whole.

**N.T. Wright** insists that Paul's salvation language -- sacrifice, redemption, justification, reconciliation, victory -- comprises distinct metaphors drawn from different social worlds that must not be collapsed. He specifically criticizes the tendency to "simply correlate all O.T. sacrifice into the penal substitutionary metaphor."

**Ted Grimsrud** (*Instead of Atonement: The Bible's Salvation Story and Our Hope for Wholeness*, Cascade Books, 2013) argues that the satisfaction view "denies that God is love, denies that mercy is life's fundamental truth, and may foster fearfulness and legalism." He traces a direct line from the theological claim that God requires sacrificial violence to justify forgiveness to real-world cultures of retributive justice. Grimsrud advocates replacing Christianity's "logic of retribution" with "a logic of mercy."

**S. Mark Heim** (*Saved from Sacrifice: A Theology of the Cross*, 2006), drawing on Rene Girard's scapegoating theory, argues that the cross reveals and shatters the human pattern of sacrificial violence rather than fulfilling it. Heim contends that reducing "the Atonement" to a single substitutionary transaction misses the fact that Jesus' death exposes something about human violence, not merely something about divine justice.

**Gustaf Aulen** (1879-1977) argued in *Christus Victor* (1930/1931) that the satisfaction and penal-substitutionary models -- both products of Western scholastic theology -- had displaced the "classic" view of the early church (Christus Victor) and introduced a misleading systematization that the Fathers never intended. The early church's view was "not so much a rational systematic theory as it is a drama."

**The Catholic Encyclopedia** itself, hardly a radical source, concludes: "It may be said, indeed, that they all help to bring out different aspects of that great doctrine which cannot find adequate expression in any human theory."

## Summary of Key Findings

1. **The English word "atonement" is a 16th-century invention** that maps a single English term onto multiple Hebrew and Greek words with distinct meanings (covering/purging, reconciliation, propitiation, redemption, justification). The word itself is a bundling device.

2. **The Bible does not present a single "Atonement."** The Old Testament sacrificial system involves at least five distinct types of offering with different purposes, and Yom Kippur itself involves multiple distinct operations (blood purification and sin-removal via the scapegoat). The New Testament uses at least six distinct Greek terms drawn from different social worlds (temple, courtroom, slave market, household).

3. **For the first thousand years of Christianity, there was no unified atonement theory.** The Church Fathers employed multiple complementary frameworks (recapitulation, ransom, Christus Victor, mystical deification) without systematizing them into a single doctrine.

4. **Anselm's *Cur Deus Homo* (1098) was the first attempt at a systematic, unified theory.** Before Anselm, there was pluralism; after Anselm, Western Christianity increasingly felt the need for a single controlling explanation.

5. **The Reformation produced the penal substitutionary model**, which became dominant in Protestantism. Catholicism and Eastern Orthodoxy rejected it as the controlling framework, maintaining older pluralistic approaches.

6. **In LDS theology, "the Atonement" as a central organizing concept is substantially a late 20th-century development.** Joseph Smith emphasized the crucifixion and resurrection. The Gethsemane emphasis developed from the 1880s through the 1980s. The phrase "atonement of Jesus Christ" quadrupled in General Conference frequency between 1942 and 2012. The 2024 volume *Latter-day Saint Perspectives on Atonement* represents the first major scholarly reckoning with this history.

7. **LDS scholar T. Benjamin Spackman has shown** that the Hebrew terms for "atone," "redeem," and "save" referred to distinct operations (ritual purification, kinship obligation, and military victory) that modern English usage -- and modern LDS usage especially -- has collapsed into interchangeable synonyms.

8. **Multiple theologians across traditions** have argued that treating "the Atonement" as a single, unified act is theologically misleading. The biblical, historical, and linguistic evidence points toward a plurality of divine operations that resist reduction to a single model or a single event.
