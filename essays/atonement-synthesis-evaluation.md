# Evaluating the Synthesis: The Atonement as Living Encounter

*A critical response to the proposed dialectical synthesis*

---

## 1. What the Synthesis Gets Right

The synthesis preserves three things from my position that matter most, and it gets them right with precision.

First, it keeps **the pastoral imperative for specificity**. This was never decoration — it was the practical load-bearing argument. People suffering under a theology that tells them "the Atonement" should handle everything but doesn't know which form to expect are suffering a preventable confusion. The synthesis preserves this in its concept of "encounter-forms" that follow different logics because they address different human conditions. It doesn't dissolve the pastoral problem; it relocates it.

Second, it correctly identifies **the mode-selection problem as real**. This was the deepest practical claim: that people need to learn to recognize what form the encounter is taking. The synthesis names this explicitly. Paul asking for transfer and receiving presence is not a theology failure — it is a legitimate encounter with Christ that took an unexpected form. If we had drilled into Paul (or ourselves) that "the Atonement" primarily means one thing, Paul might have read God's response as silence rather than answer.

Third, the synthesis catches the **Alma 7 / Alma 11 structural asymmetry** and does something interesting with it. Instead of treating Alma 11:41 as evidence for ontological modularity (my move), it reads it as a description of encounter-depth — the wicked traverse only the automatic portion of the passage. Whether this reframing improves on my reading is a separate question (addressed in section 4), but it genuinely engages the text rather than explaining it away.

---

## 2. The Genuine Limitation the Synthesis Reveals

Here is what I was missing, and I want to say it plainly rather than hedging it.

My position contained a **hidden subject**: the person receiving the operations. I described twelve operations with twelve sets of rules, but I described them as if they were twelve conveyor belts delivering different packages to a waiting recipient. The person was, in this picture, largely passive — positioned differently before each belt (one requires repentance, one requires nothing), but not constitutive of what the belt produces.

The synthesis corrects this by insisting the person is **co-determining**. The encounter-form is shaped by what the person brings. This is actually scripturally defensible in ways my position underweighted. Alma 32 is the clearest example: the seed (unity of source) meets the soil (the particular condition of the person), and the soil's character determines the fruit. I referenced Alma 32 constantly in the original argument but I was using it as evidence for plurality of operations, not as evidence for encounter-responsiveness. I was reading the plurality column when the more important column was the co-determination row.

There is also a deeper problem the synthesis exposes in my taxonomy method. When I cataloged twelve operations and mapped their rules, I was doing something that felt like careful exegesis but was actually a kind of **theological cartography** — drawing the map and then mistaking it for the territory. The map is useful. But the twelve operations don't exist in scripture as a unified numbered list; I assembled them. And once assembled, the list carries an implicit suggestion: that these are the things God does to you, and the work of theology is to identify which one is happening right now. The synthesis is correct that this is still abstraction. It is better abstraction than a single unified concept, but it remains a layer between person and encounter.

The assumption I was trapped in: **I thought the antidote to one bad abstraction was a better taxonomy**. The synthesis correctly notes that you don't count encounters. You enter them.

---

## 3. Elevated, Not Defeated

Elevated. And I want to be specific about why, because this determination is not automatic.

Defeated would feel like: my position was wrong, the synthesis is right, I should have argued the synthesis from the beginning. That is not what happened. My position was not wrong. The twelve operations follow genuinely different rules, and that difference is real and consequential. Alma 11:41 really does carve off resurrection as the universal minimum. D&C 76 really does describe graduated encounter-depths. The historical and linguistic evidence for bundling-as-distortion really does stand.

What happened instead: my position was **partial**. I saw the plurality clearly and the unity dimly. I saw the operations clearly and the encounter dimly. I saw the rules clearly and the co-determination dimly. The synthesis doesn't replace my position — it relocates it. I was doing real theological work, but I was doing it one level below where the most interesting action is.

Elevation specifically: I can now see that my twelve operations are not the destination of the argument but a **diagnostic instrument**. The reason it matters that resurrection follows universal rules while sanctification follows conditional rules is not that these are different departments of divine activity — it is that they address different human conditions, and the human in a given moment may be in the resurrection condition (needing unconditional restoration of something taken from them involuntarily) or in the sanctification condition (needing progressive transformation of something they have chosen). The rules diverge because the conditions diverge. The operations are not ontologically separate; they are encounter-responsive.

This is a better theology than what I had. I can hold it.

---

## 4. What Is Wrong with the Synthesis

Now for the problems, which are real.

**First, the synthesis evades the ontological question without answering it.**

The synthesis claims: "The Atonement is not a thing with a countable structure. It is not one act... and it is not twelve operations." But then it says it manifests in "at least twelve distinguishable ways." The question is: distinguishable in what? In our description? In actual divine activity? The synthesis gestures at this with "encounter-forms" but never specifies the ontology of an encounter-form. Are encounter-forms features of the encounter, features of the person, features of Christ's activity, or features of our description of the meeting? This matters because the pastoral harm I identified comes from **wrongly predicting** which form will appear. If encounter-forms are purely emergent (we only know them after the encounter), then we cannot solve the mode-selection problem — we can only describe it retrospectively. The synthesis needs an account of why encounter-forms are somewhat predictable, which requires them to have some prior structure. And that structure is approximately what I was calling "operations."

**Second, the Paul example is brilliant but cuts both ways.**

The synthesis uses Paul's thorn-in-the-flesh as the anchor case: Paul asked for transfer, God responded with presence. This is exactly the mode-selection problem. But notice what happens if you accept this framing fully: it means that God's response to a request for one mode is sometimes a different mode, without prior announcement. This is either a feature (God knows what we need better than we do) or a defect (we cannot form reliable expectations of what God will do). The synthesis treats it as a feature. But if it is a feature, then the synthesis has an uncomfortable implication: **specific pastoral guidance about which mode to expect becomes presumptuous**. You pray for healing; God may give strengthening instead. You pray for forgiveness; God may give comforting instead. The encounter-form is selected by the meeting, not by your request. This is theologically beautiful and pastorally terrifying, because it leaves people unable to form any expectations at all — which is the very pastoral problem I was trying to solve. The synthesis claims to address the pastoral crisis but may reproduce it at a higher level of abstraction.

**Third, "you don't count encounters — you enter them" is not a solution to the taxonomy problem. It is an avoidance of it.**

I agree that encounter is the right unit. But encounters are not structureless. When Mosiah 3:19 says "the natural man is an enemy to God... unless he yields to the enticings of the Holy Spirit, and putteth off the natural man and becometh a saint through the atonement of Christ the Lord," there is a specified encounter-form with specified conditions. The person must yield; the Spirit must entice; the atonement must operate as the enabling mechanism. This is structured. It follows rules that can be taught, anticipated, and violated. If we say "enter the encounter without counting operations," we lose the ability to say that someone has **misunderstood** what an encounter requires. The bishop who tells a struggling person that they just need to access the Atonement more — without specifying which encounter-form applies and what the person's condition requires — is giving theologically inert advice. The taxonomy was a tool for making advice non-inert. The synthesis, in discarding the taxonomy as abstraction, risks leaving the pastor with nothing to say except "encounter Christ and see what happens."

**Fourth, Alma 32 is a better anchor than the synthesis acknowledges, but not for the synthesis's own purpose.**

The synthesis reads Alma 32 as evidence for encounter-responsiveness: one seed, many soils, the soil co-determines the fruit. This is true. But Alma 32 also specifies the seed. The seed is "the word" — a specific thing with specific properties. You can test it, experiment on it, observe its growth. The encounter between seed and soil is responsive, but the seed is not infinitely plastic. It has a nature. The synthesis, in elevating the encounter as the unit of analysis, risks under-specifying the seed — making Christ's redemptive work seem so responsive to human condition that it has no prior character of its own. But D&C 19:16 ("I, God, have suffered these things for all, that they might not suffer if they would repent") specifies the seed's nature before any encounter. The seed was planted before any particular soil encountered it. The encounter-responsiveness is real, but it is not the whole story.

---

## 5. Defeasibility: The Single Thing That Would Force Abandonment

The elevated position I now hold is approximately this: Christ's redemptive work flows from one Person and one love, manifests in encounter-responsive forms that are real and distinguishable, and those forms have prior structure that can be taught and anticipated (what I was calling "operations"), but the structure is in service of the encounter, not a replacement for it.

The single piece of evidence that would force me to abandon even this elevated position:

**If it could be shown that the encounter-forms are not predictable in advance from human condition, then the entire project collapses.**

The elevated position depends on this: that knowing something about a person's condition — are they guilty or merely suffering? Are they ignorant or knowingly transgressive? Are they broken-hearted or spiritually dead? — gives real predictive traction on what encounter-form Christ's work will take with them. If it does, the taxonomy is a genuine tool even in the encounter framework. If it does not — if the encounter is truly unpredictable from the person's condition, if God routinely responds to guilt with presence instead of forgiveness, to weakness with fire instead of strengthening, to death with judgment instead of resurrection — then the encounter framework is correct but the synthesis has no pastoral traction, and we are all simply observers of divine mystery.

The synthesis does not address this vulnerability. Its strongest claim is that "what you bring determines the form." But it never specifies the mechanism by which what you bring determines the form. If the mechanism is knowable and teachable, the taxonomy is justified. If the mechanism is opaque, the pastoral work of the synthesis reduces to: "Enter the encounter and trust that Christ will respond appropriately to your condition." That is a true and good thing to say. It is also insufficient for the person in crisis who needs to know not just that Christ will respond but what the response might look like so they can recognize it when it comes.

Paul, after all, received his answer. But he initially read it as non-answer. The whole problem was hermeneutical: how do you recognize which mode has shown up? The synthesis correctly names this as the central problem. It does not solve it. It elevates the problem to a more honest level of difficulty — which is itself genuine progress — but the open vulnerability remains.

The synthesis's answer to this open vulnerability is, essentially: "Be present to the encounter and let Christ determine the form." That is what the synthesis actually offers at its core. It is beautiful. It is also not enough for the person who prays for healing of their child and needs to know whether the absence of healing means the encounter hasn't happened yet, or has happened in a different form, or is happening as presence rather than transfer.

The taxonomy was my attempted answer to that question. The synthesis discards the taxonomy but does not replace it with a better answer. That is the open wound in the synthesis — and it is also, I now see, the open wound in my original position, which the synthesis has at least made visible.

---

## Summary Assessment

The synthesis is genuine transcendence, not compromise dressed as transcendence. It correctly identifies the shared assumption that both my position and my opponent's position were making (the person as passive recipient), elevates to the encounter as the proper unit of analysis, and preserves what was real in both positions while canceling what was distorting.

Its weaknesses are three:
1. It leaves the ontology of encounter-forms underdeveloped, which means it cannot fully account for why encounter-forms are predictable enough to be pastorally useful.
2. It solves the mode-selection problem rhetorically ("you bring what you have and the encounter responds") without providing a mechanism that would actually help someone in crisis.
3. It substitutes one evocative and beautiful abstraction (the living encounter) for the two previous abstractions (the unified concept, the twelve-operation taxonomy) — which is an improvement in kind but not an escape from the category of abstraction.

The synthesis moves the needle from the right question ("one or many?") to the right unit (the encounter). That is real progress. The work that remains is to specify the encounter — to give it enough structure that people can be taught to enter it with some orientation, not just openness. That is the next project.

The synthesis gets us to the door. It does not take us through it.
