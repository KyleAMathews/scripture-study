# Phase 5: Round 3 Determinate Negation — The Metaphor Landscape

## 5.0 Internal Tensions

### Monk A (Developmental) undermines itself

Monk A's most powerful evidence is the temple endowment — a fixed, ordered, non-reversible sequence of covenants. But the temple itself contains a devastating counter-example to the developmental thesis: **the endowment accumulates covenants rather than replacing them.** Monk B catches this correctly. When you receive the endowment, you do not stop being under the baptismal covenant. When you are sealed, you do not graduate from the endowment. The temple's own logic is accumulative, not sequential-replacement. Monk A needs the metaphors to be stages where later ones supersede earlier ones, but the temple — Monk A's best evidence — preserves all stages simultaneously.

Also: Monk A's ordering of the metaphors along "an axis of increasing mutuality" is constructed, not discovered. Why does physician come after shepherd? Why does vine come after father? Monk A treats the ordering as self-evident, but the ordering was imposed by the argument, not extracted from scripture. No scriptural text arranges these metaphors in this sequence. The temple arranges covenants in sequence, but it does not arrange *metaphors for God* in sequence. Monk A has confused the ordering of covenantal commitments with the ordering of relational modes — these are not the same thing.

And the hardest internal problem: **D&C 93 cuts both ways.** Monk A cites "from grace to grace" as proof of developmental structure. But "from grace to grace" is exactly what the word says — grace to grace. Not "from merit to greater merit" or "from stage to stage." The movement is through *received grace*, not through *earned developmental transitions*. The very text Monk A uses as evidence for developmental structure actually emphasizes the priority of grace at every point. Development happens — but within a grace-saturated environment, not as an achievement unlocking the next level.

### Monk B (Pluralist) undermines itself

Monk B's strongest claim is that the metaphors are irreducible — later ones don't subsume earlier ones, they coexist permanently. This is genuinely true for the metaphors as *descriptions*. But Monk B then makes a theological inference that doesn't follow: because the descriptions are irreducible, God's relational engagement must be structureless. Monk A catches this correctly — it's the fallacy of inferring from irreducibility of description to irreducibility of structure.

Monk B also overplays the simultaneity argument. Yes, Isaiah 64:8 puts potter and father in the same sentence. Yes, John 15 puts vine and friend in the same discourse. But Monk B treats simultaneity as disproof of development, when it could equally be evidence of something else: **that a mature relationship retains the memory and reality of all earlier modes while operating in new ones.** My marriage to my wife contains the reality of our first date, our courtship, our wedding, and our mature partnership — all simultaneously present. That doesn't mean our relationship has no developmental structure. It means that development *accumulates* rather than *replaces*. Monk B sees this accumulation and concludes "no structure." A better conclusion is "the structure is accumulative, not sequential-replacement."

Monk B's deepest failure: the "idolatry" charge is unfalsifiable and cuts both ways. If seeking structure in God's self-disclosure is idolatry (replacing the living God with a model), then Monk B's own position — "the plurality is itself the revelation" — is equally a model. You've just made the model "there is no model," which is still a model. The charge of idolatry is a rhetorical escalation, not an argument.

## 5.1 Surface Contradiction

God's scriptural metaphors describe a single developmental trajectory with covenant marking the transitions vs. God's metaphors are irreducible simultaneous modes with no master structure.

## 5.2 Shared Assumptions

1. **Both assume the relevant unit is the individual person.** Both monks analyze how God relates to *a person* — singular. Neither considers that most of God's metaphors operate at the COMMUNAL level. The shepherd has a *flock*. The vine has *branches* (plural). The bridegroom has a *bride* (the church, not an individual). The potter works with *Israel*, not just a person. The developmental trajectory and the pluralist landscape are both individual-scale analyses of what scripture describes at communal scale.

2. **Both assume the relationship is direct: God ↔ person.** Neither considers that God's relationship with most humans is MEDIATED through the covenant community. The narrow way essay identifies this: "The covenant people function as instruments, not endpoints. They are the salt of the earth, the light on a hill, the witnesses to all nations." The shepherd seeks the lost sheep — but in practice, who does the seeking? The covenant people, functioning as under-shepherds. The vine feeds the branches — but in the actual church, the branch draws life through other branches as well as through the vine. The metaphors describe not just God's direct relationship with individuals but God's relationship with humanity THROUGH a mediating community.

3. **Both abstract away the SCALE of the problem.** This is the critical missed dimension. The narrow way essay identifies it with uncomfortable clarity: "Strait is the gate, and narrow is the way, which leadeth unto life, and few there be that find it" (Matthew 7:14). The vast majority of God's children have NEVER been in formal covenant. They don't know God personally. They have never heard the shepherd's voice in any covenantal sense. They have never entered the developmental trajectory. They have never experienced the plurality of relational modes. Their entire mortal experience of God is mediated by the light of Christ — the most basic, pre-personal, universal mode. Both monks are debating the interior architecture of a relationship that describes the *minority* experience. Neither asks: what is the structure of God's relationship with the *majority* of His children?

4. **Both treat the metaphors as STATIC descriptions.** Neither considers that the metaphors might describe God's *strategy* for reaching all His children — a strategy that works through a small covenant people who expand outward. The leaven metaphor, the mustard seed, the salt: these are not descriptions of a relationship. They are descriptions of a *process* — something small that permeates and transforms the whole. The narrow way essay: "The smallness is not failure but design."

## 5.3 Determinate Negation

- **Monk A fails** because the developmental trajectory only describes the experience of the covenant minority. If most humans never enter the trajectory in mortality, then development is a local description (how the participatory cycle deepens for those inside it), not a universal structure. The temple is real, the progression is real, but it describes the *instrument's* experience, not the universal pattern. This failure reveals: Monk A needs a framework for how the developmental trajectory extends to the vast majority who don't enter it in mortality.

- **Monk B fails** because irreducible plurality without structure degenerates into "God does whatever." If the modes are truly structureless and situational, then theology of faith is impossible — you can never say anything general about how God relates to humans, because it's always "it depends." The irreducibility is real but it needs a governing logic. This failure reveals: Monk B needs a framework for how the plurality serves a PURPOSE — not just "God is rich" but "God's richness works toward a specific end."

The failures are complementary: A has structure without universality. B has universality (God meets everyone where they are) without structure. Both need a framework that is both structured AND universal — that explains how God's relationship with the covenant few serves God's saving purpose for ALL.

## 5.4 The Hidden Question

Not "are the metaphors developmental stages or irreducible modes?" but **"what is the ECONOMY of salvation — how does God's multi-modal relationship with a small covenant people serve His universal purpose to save all His children?"**

The monks are debating the interior furnishings of the house. The real question is about the house's function in the neighborhood. The covenant people are not the endpoint of God's relational engagement. They are the *instrument* through which God reaches everyone else. The metaphors don't just describe how God relates to individuals — they describe the ROLES that covenant people play in God's strategy for reaching the whole human family.

The shepherd metaphor: God seeks the lost. In practice, He does this THROUGH under-shepherds — missionaries, ministers, parents, friends. The covenant community IS the shepherd's staff extended into the world.

The light metaphor: God illuminates. In practice, He does this through "a light unto the Gentiles" (Isaiah 49:6) — the covenant people functioning as transmitted light. D&C 115:5: "let your light so shine."

The leaven metaphor: something small permeates the whole. The covenant community IS the leaven. The few who enter covenant are meant to transform the many who haven't — not by replacing their experience with covenant but by creating conditions where more can encounter and enter.

The physician metaphor: God heals. The church IS the hospital — "whosoever repenteth and cometh unto me, the same is my church" (D&C 10:67). Not a club for the healed but a net for gathering the sick.

## 5.5 Boydian Decomposition

### Generic space
Both monks share a "God relates to X" model where X is a single individual and the question is about the structure of the relationship. This is the skeleton to transcend.

### Atomic components (stripped from positions)
- Scripture uses 8+ distinct metaphors for God's relational engagement
- The metaphors differ in human agency, divine initiative, and mutuality
- The temple presents covenants in ordered, accumulative sequence
- D&C 93: "from grace to grace" — development is real
- John 15: vine AND friend in the same discourse — simultaneity is real
- Isaiah 64:8: potter AND father in the same sentence — accumulation is real
- Jacob 5: Master uses multiple strategies adaptively
- Revelation 7:17: shepherd at eschatological climax — modes persist
- Mosiah 5:7: covenant ENTERS the parent-child metaphor
- John 15:15: "henceforth" — temporal marker of relational shift
- D&C 84: servant → son → friend linked to covenant
- Alma 32: development within a single metaphor (seed → tree)
- The narrow way: few find the path, the rest are outside
- Covenant people as instruments: salt, light, leaven, witnesses
- God's timetable extends beyond mortality: spirit world, vicarious work
- "Whosoever repenteth and cometh unto me, the same is my church" — the church as gathering net

### Surprising cross-connections

- **Jacob 5's Master + the narrow way essay**: Jacob 5 is not just about the developmental trajectory of individual trees. It is about the ECONOMY of the vineyard — the Master's strategy for producing fruit across the WHOLE vineyard. He grafts wild branches into tame trees. He scatters tame branches into wild trees. He plants in different spots of ground. The allegory is about DISTRIBUTION — how the covenant fruit reaches every corner of the garden. This is the economy of salvation enacted in olive tree metaphor. Jacob 5 supports both monks partially: it shows development (the trees mature over time) AND plurality (different trees get different treatment). But primarily it shows something NEITHER monk considers: a God strategically working THROUGH a covenant people to reach all His children.

- **"Salt of the earth" + "without me ye can do nothing"**: Salt that has lost its savor is "thenceforth good for nothing" (Matthew 5:13). The covenant people's entire purpose IS to be instrument of transformation for others. If they turn inward — if they treat the covenant as endpoint rather than commission — they lose their function. The vine/branches metaphor (without me ye can do nothing) applies not just to individual-God relationship but to the covenant community's relationship with its mission. The community draws life from God (vine) in order to produce fruit (outward transformation). The developmental trajectory exists to CREATE INSTRUMENTS, not to produce finished individuals.

- **D&C 128:15 + the pre-covenant problem**: "They without us cannot be made perfect — neither can we without them be made perfect." This demolishes both monks' individual framing. The developmental trajectory is not an individual journey — it is a COMMUNAL economy where the covenant-keeping of the living extends salvation to the dead, and the acceptance by the dead completes the work of the living. The metaphors are not about God's relationship with persons but about God's relationship with a CONNECTED NETWORK of persons, living and dead, covenant and pre-covenant, who together constitute the body of Christ.

- **The "remnant strategy" + Mosiah 3:19's bootstrapping problem**: How does someone outside covenant begin the yielding? The narrow way essay gives the answer: through CONTACT with the covenant people functioning as instruments. The missionary is the extended arm of the shepherd. The faithful neighbor is the transmitted light. The temple worker is the physician operating on behalf of the dead. The pre-covenantal grace (light of Christ) creates the CONDITIONS for receptivity, and the covenant community creates the OCCASIONS for encounter. The bootstrapping paradox dissolves when you stop thinking individually: the covenant community IS the mechanism by which pre-covenantal people enter covenant.

### Adjacent-domain material

**The body of Christ (1 Corinthians 12)**: Paul's metaphor of the church as a body with many members and many functions. The eye cannot say to the hand "I have no need of you." This is irreducible plurality WITH structure — many modes, one body, one purpose. Paul's body metaphor is the scriptural answer to the developmental-vs-pluralist debate: the modes are genuinely different (eye ≠ hand), they coexist simultaneously (not developmental stages), AND they serve a unified purpose (the body's health). The body is structured but not linearly — it is an ORGANISM with differentiated parts serving a common telos.

**The gathering of Israel**: The Restoration's central narrative is not about individual spiritual development. It is about the GATHERING — collecting the covenant people, building Zion, performing vicarious work, extending the covenant to all who have ever lived. The metaphors describe roles within this gathering economy, not just individual relational modes.

**LDS temple work for the dead**: The most distinctive feature of LDS covenant theology is that covenants extend BEYOND the living. The temple performs vicarious ordinances for those who never had access to covenant in mortality. This means the developmental trajectory IS universal — not because everyone enters it in mortality, but because the economy of salvation eventually extends it to everyone through the mediating work of the covenant community. The answer to "what about the 99% outside covenant?" is: the 1% inside covenant are commissioned to bring the covenant to them, in this life and the next.

## 5.6 Sublation Criteria

- Must preserve Monk A's insight: the relational metaphors have developmental structure — there IS progression, covenants DO mark transitions, the temple's ordering is real
- Must preserve Monk B's insight: the metaphors are genuinely plural and not reducible to a single linear sequence — they coexist, they do different work, no single metaphor captures everything
- Must dissolve: the shared assumption that the metaphors describe God's relationship with individuals. They describe God's ECONOMY of salvation working through a covenant community
- Must answer: how do God's multiple relational modes serve His universal purpose to save all His children, given that most never enter formal covenant in mortality?
- Must engage: the narrow way essay's insight that the covenant people are instruments, not endpoints — salt, light, leaven — and that God's timetable extends beyond mortality
- Must account for: D&C 128:15 — "they without us cannot be made perfect, neither can we without them" — the communal interdependence of salvation
