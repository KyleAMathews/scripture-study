# Phase 6 (Revised): Sublation — Justice and Mercy as One Reality Differently Received

## The Synthesis

Neither the courtroom nor the household is the fire. Both are shadows. But the deeper move: **even calling them "two attributes" is a mortal artifact.** Justice and mercy are not two divine properties in tension. They are a single divine reality — God's perfect love, expressed through His perfect alignment with the nature of intelligence, truth, and agency — that is *experienced differently* depending on the receiver's orientation.

The same God. The same love. The same presence. Received as glory by the one who turns homeward. Received as consuming fire by the one who has chosen darkness.

### Why This Is Not Compromise

This is not "God is both just and merciful" (the Sunday School answer). This is: **justice and mercy are not separate things.** The separation is an artifact of mortal cognition — we can only think of one thing at a time (Nibley's point in "Zeal Without Knowledge"), so we experience God's single, unified nature as two opposing attributes that need reconciling. God does not experience this tension because His mind is not partitioned the way ours is. "My thoughts are not your thoughts, neither are your ways my ways" (Isaiah 55:8) is not just about moral superiority — it is about a fundamentally different mode of being that cannot be captured in mortal categories.

### The Argument

**God is supreme among co-eternal intelligences, not subject to external law.** Abraham 3:17-19 is clear: "there is nothing that the Lord thy God shall take in his heart to do but what he will do it" and "I am more intelligent than they all." But intelligences are co-eternal (Abraham 3:18) and truth is independent in its sphere (D&C 93:29-30). God perfectly embodies and understands the nature of these co-eternal realities. His Godhood is not servitude to a law above Him — it is perfect alignment with the nature of things. This is what "God would cease to be God" means: not that an external law would deconstruct Him, but that perfect alignment with the nature of intelligence, truth, and love IS what Godhood is. Misalignment would be a contradiction in terms.

**What we call "justice" is how God's love is experienced by the one who has chosen separation.** To the prodigal in the far country, the Father's nature feels like unyielding law — "you cannot come home like this." But this is not law imposed from outside. It is love's intrinsic refusal to call self-destruction acceptable. A love that could look upon the beloved's destruction with allowance would not be perfect love. D&C 1:31 — "I the Lord cannot look upon sin with the least degree of allowance" — is not a legal constraint. It is a description of what perfect love IS. Sin is the thing destroying the beloved; love that tolerated it would be defective love.

**What we call "mercy" is how God's love is experienced by the one who turns homeward.** The same Father, the same love, the same nature — but now received as embrace rather than as unyielding standard. Nothing changes in God. Everything changes in the child's orientation. The prodigal turns, and the father runs — not because mercy has now "overridden" justice, but because the child's reorientation means the same love is now experienced as rescue rather than as reproach.

**The Atonement is how God's love enters into the full reality of what separation costs.** It is not a payment to a cosmic creditor (Monk A's claim). It is not merely a parent's gesture of solidarity (Monk B's claim — which can't explain necessity). It is what happens when perfect empathy, perfectly aligned with the nature of things, encounters the full weight of what self-destruction does to beings God loves. Christ descends below all things (D&C 122:8) because that is what perfect love DOES when confronted with the beloved's suffering. The necessity in D&C 19:16-17 ("they must suffer even as I") is not legal necessity — it is the necessity inherent in the nature of intelligence and agency: consequences are real because agency is real, and someone must experience the full weight of those consequences. Christ's love drives Him to absorb that weight Himself.

**The sons of perdition are not abandoned by love — they cannot tolerate it.** Brigham Young taught that the wicked forced into God's presence would find it "more excruciating and unendurable than to dwell in the lake that burns with fire and brimstone." Joseph Smith taught that "a man is his own tormentor and his own condemner" and that "God would not exert any compulsory means." D&C 29:44-45: "they cannot be redeemed from their spiritual fall, because they repent not; for they love darkness rather than light." God's love does not stop at the boundary of perdition. But the sons of perdition have so fully chosen darkness that light itself is torment. The celestial kingdom — God's unfiltered presence — would be worse than fire for them. Justice here is not God withholding mercy. It is mercy itself, experienced as agony by those who have made themselves allergic to it.

This resolves the Joseph Smith quote perfectly: God IS "more liberal in His views, and boundless in His mercies" AND "more terrible to the workers of iniquity" — not because He toggles between two modes, but because **His love is the same infinite force that registers as boundless mercy to those who want to be good and as terrible judgment to those who have chosen iniquity.** Both dials are turned to the same setting. The difference is in the receiver, not the sender.

### What This Does to the Scriptural Frameworks

**Alma 42** is Alma translating this unified reality into legal terms for Corianton — and doing a remarkably good job. "God would cease to be God" captures something true: God's nature has structural necessities that flow from what perfect love IS. But the legal metaphor (demands, claims, satisfaction, payment) is a projection of the deeper reality into mortal categories. Alma reaches for courtroom language because Corianton is trying to lawyer his way out of guilt, and the courtroom frame communicates the seriousness of what he has done. The legal frame is not FALSE — it captures real structural features of divine love. But it is not the thing itself.

**Moses 7** is closer to the fire. When God weeps AND sends floods simultaneously, He is not toggling between justice and mercy. He is being one thing — perfectly loving — and that one thing produces both the grief and the consequences. Enoch's confusion ("How can you weep?") is the confusion of someone reasoning from the legal frame: an eternal, just God should not weep. God's answer dissolves the frame: "they are the workmanship of mine own hands... they should choose me, their Father." The answer is parental because the reality IS more like parenthood than like jurisprudence — but it exceeds even the parental frame, because a mortal parent doesn't simultaneously cause and grieve the child's suffering the way God does in Moses 7.

**D&C 19** is God pulling back the curtain on His own pedagogical accommodations: the terrifying legal language ("eternal punishment," "endless torment") is real in describing the quality of the experience but is deliberately framed to "work upon the hearts of the children of men." God is telling us: the legal-sounding frame serves a purpose, but the purpose is relational — to move human hearts — not descriptive of the underlying architecture.

**The Prodigal Son** is the closest single parable to the actual structure: the father's love never changes. The son's orientation changes. The consequences of departure are real (pig slop, starvation) but they are inherent in the separation, not imposed by a code. And the moment the son turns, the father runs. No legal proceeding. No mediator. No payment. Just love, unfiltered, rushing to close the distance.

### The Cognitive Limitation (Nibley's Key)

Why do the scriptures use BOTH frames if the reality is one thing? Because of what Nibley identifies in "Zeal Without Knowledge": mortal minds process one thing at a time. We cannot simultaneously hold "unyielding structural necessity" and "boundless tender compassion" as a single unified reality. So the scriptures give us TWO projections — the legal frame for when we need to grasp seriousness, consequences, and necessity; the relational frame for when we need to grasp love, tenderness, and hope. Both are true. Neither is complete. The complete picture is something our mortal cognition cannot hold in a single thought — which is exactly what "my ways are not your ways" means.

This is the Platonic cave the user identified. We sit in mortal cognition, watching shadows on the wall — the shadow of the courtroom, the shadow of the household — and we argue about which shadow is "really real." The fire that casts both shadows is a unified divine nature that we cannot look at directly with mortal minds. The scriptures give us shadows because shadows are what we can see.

## New Contradictions Generated

1. **The development problem:** If God's Godhood IS His perfect alignment with the nature of things, does this mean God developed this alignment over time? (King Follett discourse, Lorenzo Snow couplet: "As man now is, God once was.") If God "became" perfectly loving, what was He before? And does this mean the "nature of things" (intelligence, truth, agency) is more fundamental than God after all?

2. **The pedagogy problem:** If the legal frame is pedagogical scaffolding, what happens when the scaffolding is taken too literally? Has the legal frame in LDS culture produced distortions — legalistic approaches to worthiness, shame-based repentance, a transactional view of the Atonement that actually hinders the relationship it's meant to serve?

3. **The agency limit:** If God's love is the same force regardless of receiver, and the difference is entirely in the receiver's orientation — what determines orientation? Is it purely volitional (the receiver freely chooses)? If so, why do some choose darkness? Can a being with perfect information choose self-destruction? What does this imply about the nature of agency among co-eternal intelligences?

## Model Update

- **Before:** Justice and mercy are two divine attributes in tension, reconciled by the Atonement.
- **After:** Justice and mercy are mortal-cognitive projections of a single divine reality — God's perfect love, flowing from His supreme alignment with the nature of intelligence, truth, and agency. The "tension" between them is an artifact of mortal minds that can only process one thing at a time. The same divine love registers as unyielding justice to those who have chosen separation and as boundless mercy to those who turn homeward. The Atonement is what perfect love does when it encounters the full weight of the beloved's self-destruction — not a legal payment, not merely a relational healing, but love entering into suffering because that is what love IS. The sons of perdition are not abandoned by love; they are tormented by it, because they have made themselves unable to receive what God endlessly offers.
- **Because:** Both the legal frame and the relational frame fail in complementary ways that point to a reality beyond either. The legal frame cannot explain a God who weeps at His own verdicts (Moses 7) or who tells us His punitive language is pedagogical (D&C 19). The relational frame cannot explain why suffering is necessary (D&C 19:16-17) or why God "would cease to be God" (Alma 42). But if both frames are projections of a single reality — perfect love with structural necessity built into its nature — then both failures dissolve. God weeps because love grieves. Suffering is necessary because love must enter into the beloved's reality. God would cease to be God because love that tolerated destruction would not be love. The fire casts both shadows.
