# The Atonement Is Not One Thing

## Monk B (Plurality) -- Round 5 Argument

---

## The Ontological Claim: What the Bundling Obscures

"The Atonement" is a sixteenth-century English word doing twenty-first-century theological work it was never designed for. Tyndale coined it in 1526 to translate *katallag&eacute;* -- Greek for reconciliation between estranged parties -- and then redeployed the same word for Hebrew *kaphar*, which meant ritual purging of contamination from sacred space. These are not the same operation. One restores a relationship. The other scrubs pollution off an altar. Tyndale papered over the difference, and we have been living inside that papering-over ever since.

The problem is not merely linguistic. The problem is that when you call twelve distinct divine operations by one name, you import the logic of whichever operation you happen to be thinking about into all the others -- and the logic does not transfer. Consider the rules:

Resurrection is universal, unconditional, and requires nothing from the recipient. Forgiveness is conditional, requires repentance and a broken heart, and applies only to the penitent. Sanctification is progressive, requires sustained effort and the Holy Ghost, and unfolds over a lifetime. Succoring addresses suffering rather than sin, requires only need, and operates in present tense without any connection to a specific historical moment. The perfection of the saints demands maximal human effort enabled by maximal grace. The salvation of children cancels inherited guilt preemptively and requires nothing at all.

These operations do not follow one logic. They follow six different logics. Calling them all "the Atonement" generates the expectation that one explanatory framework -- whether penal substitution, or compassion theory, or moral influence -- will account for the whole. It will not. It cannot. And the attempt to force it produces the very theological confusion that four rounds of this dialectic have been trying to resolve.

Alma 11:41 says it plainly: the wicked remain "as though there had been no redemption made, except it be the loosing of the bands of death." Scripture itself carves resurrection off from everything else. The wicked get resurrection and nothing more. If the Atonement were one indivisible act producing one indivisible effect, this carve-out would be incoherent. It is not incoherent. It is modular. The scripture is telling us what we refuse to hear.

## The Opponent's Strongest Case

The unity advocate's best argument runs like this: Christ's total entry into the human condition -- his passage through mortality, suffering, death, hell, and resurrection -- constituted a single structural change in the relationship between divine and human nature. Every subsequent operation (forgiveness, transformation, succoring, resurrection) flows from that one structural change the way heat, light, and gravity all flow from one star. The operations look different because they reach different situations, but the source is one. To fragment the Atonement into separate acts is to mistake the rays for separate suns.

This is a serious argument. It honors the unity of Christ's person and the coherence of his mission. And it is wrong at the structural level.

## Why the Unity Claim Fails

The star analogy breaks precisely where it matters most: at the point of human response. Heat, light, and gravity from a star all reach you whether you want them to or not. They operate by the same physics. They require nothing from the recipient. But the Atonement's operations emphatically do not work this way. Resurrection reaches you regardless. Forgiveness reaches you only if you repent. Sanctification reaches you only if you yield your heart to God over years of effort. Exaltation reaches you only through specific covenants and ordinances. Paul asked three times for the thorn to be removed; God said no and offered presence instead of transfer.

If these were all manifestations of one structural change, why does one bypass every human condition while another demands maximal human engagement? The unity advocate must say: "The conditions are about reception, not about the source." But this concedes the point. If the *reception* follows radically different rules depending on which operation is in play, then knowing that "the source is one" tells you almost nothing useful about what is actually happening in any given person's encounter with Christ. The unity claim becomes a metaphysical fact with no pastoral utility -- true in the way it is true that all elements come from stellar nucleosynthesis, and equally unhelpful when you need to know whether to drink the water or breathe the oxygen.

The unity framework actively misleads because it generates the expectation that all operations work the same way. A person suffering from depression seeks the Atonement expecting transfer -- the removal of pain, as sins are removed. When God operates instead in presence mode (the fourth figure in the furnace, "my grace is sufficient for thee"), the person concludes that the Atonement is not working for them. This is not a hypothetical. This is the pastoral crisis described in this dialectic's own source material: the user spent years "tripped up" expecting "saved-by-grace magic" -- expecting one mode when God was operating in another. The unity label created the false expectation. Unbundling would have prevented it.

## Diagnosis: Why the Unity Reading Persists

The unity reading persists for three reasons, none of them theological.

First, historical accident. Anselm's *Cur Deus Homo* in 1098 was the first attempt at a single controlling atonement theory. Before Anselm, the Church Fathers held multiple complementary frameworks -- Christus Victor, ransom, recapitulation, theosis -- without feeling any need to systematize them into one. The felt need for ONE explanation is a medieval Western European invention, not a scriptural requirement. Eastern Orthodoxy never adopted it. The first thousand years of Christianity managed without it.

Second, linguistic convenience. As Spackman has demonstrated, the Hebrew terms *kaphar* (ritual purification), *ga'al* (kinship redemption -- the obligation of a relative to buy back family from slavery), and *yasha* (military victory, deliverance from enemies) were three operations from three different social worlds: the temple, the household, and the battlefield. English collapsed them into synonyms. Modern Latter-day Saints use "atone," "redeem," and "save" interchangeably, as though a priest sprinkling blood on the mercy seat, a kinsman paying a relative's debt, and a warrior routing an army are the same act. They are not. The synonymy is an artifact of translation, not a theological discovery.

Third, institutional momentum. "The Atonement of Jesus Christ" quadrupled in General Conference frequency between 1942 and 2012. The phrase barely appears in Joseph Smith's own teaching -- he was crucicentric, with fifteen cross references to one Gethsemane reference in the Doctrine and Covenants. "The Atonement" as a central organizing concept is substantially a late twentieth-century LDS development, driven by Talmage, Joseph Fielding Smith, McConkie, and Callister. We have mistaken a recent rhetorical convention for an eternal theological truth.

## What Becomes Visible When You Unbundle

The moment you stop calling it one thing, three critical distinctions snap into focus.

**First, the mode-selection problem becomes tractable.** The Round 4 synthesis identified three modes -- transfer, presence, and transformation -- but could not explain what determines which mode operates when. The unity framework cannot answer this question because it insists the source is one. But if these are genuinely distinct operations, then the question becomes: which operation is God performing in this situation, and what does that specific operation require of me? The answer to Paul's thorn is not "the Atonement works differently sometimes" -- it is that succoring and forgiveness are different operations with different conditions and different human responses. Paul was requesting Operation 2 (removal). God was providing Operation 5 (sustaining presence). Paul needed to recognize which operation was active, not wonder why "the Atonement" seemed inconsistent.

**Second, the resurrection/exaltation asymmetry resolves.** The Round 4 hostile auditor asked: if the passage is one structure, why does resurrection bypass the covenant protocol while exaltation requires it? The unity framework has no answer. The plurality framework does: because they are different operations. Resurrection reverses an involuntary condition (death, which happened *to* you without your consent). Exaltation addresses a volitional trajectory (becoming like God, which requires your sustained choosing). Different problems require different solutions. The asymmetry is not a puzzle to be solved but evidence of intelligent design -- distinct operations calibrated to distinct problems.

**Third, the Book of Mormon's own internal plurality becomes legible.** Spencer has shown that Benjamin and Abinadi present genuinely different atonement theologies -- Benjamin's monarchical blood-theology versus Abinadi's divine-descent theology. The unity reading must treat this as stylistic variation. The plurality reading recognizes it as the text itself preserving the distinctness of operations that later redactors and modern readers have collapsed. Alma 7:11-13 catalogs three distinct purposes of Christ's suffering (loosing death, gaining empathetic knowledge, blotting out transgressions) and -- critically -- never uses the word "atonement" for the succoring operation. The Book of Mormon's own vocabulary resists the bundling we impose on it.

## The Extreme Claim

Here is the strongest, most uncomfortable version of this thesis: **"the Atonement" as a unified concept has become an idol** -- a human-made abstraction that we have placed between ourselves and the actual operations of God. We worship the concept instead of attending to the specific thing Christ is doing in our specific situation. When someone says "I need the Atonement," that statement is so vague as to be nearly meaningless -- it is like saying "I need medicine" without specifying whether you have a broken bone or an infection. The treatment for a broken bone will not cure an infection. The treatment for sin (repentance, forgiveness) will not cure weakness (humility, grace transforming limitation into strength). The treatment for death (resurrection) requires nothing from you at all. These are different operations. Calling them one thing does not honor Christ's work. It obscures it.

D&C 76 describes three kingdoms of glory where different people receive categorically different outcomes from Christ's redemptive work. The telestial inhabitants are "heirs of salvation" who "received not the gospel, neither the testimony of Jesus." They receive resurrection and a glory surpassing all understanding -- but not exaltation. The Atonement, if it were one indivisible thing, would either apply to them fully or not at all. Instead, it applies *modularly*. Some operations reach them. Others do not. The graduated kingdoms are the Father's own testimony that what we call "the Atonement" is a portfolio of operations, not a monolith.

## The Reasoning Skeleton

1. **Premise:** Different operations that follow different rules, address different problems, and require different human responses are genuinely different operations, not manifestations of one operation.
2. **Observation:** Scripture attributes to Christ's redemptive work at least twelve operations that follow radically different rules (universal vs. conditional, completed vs. ongoing, requiring nothing vs. requiring maximal effort).
3. **Historical fact:** The bundling of these operations under one English label is a sixteenth-century translation artifact, reinforced by an eleventh-century scholastic impulse and amplified by a twentieth-century rhetorical trend.
4. **Inference:** Calling them "the Atonement" imports the logic of one operation into the others, generating false expectations about how any given operation works.
5. **Consequence:** The pastoral crisis of expecting transfer when God intends presence, or expecting automatic relief when God requires sustained transformation, is a direct product of the bundling.
6. **Conclusion:** Unbundling the operations -- while fully affirming that they flow from the same Person and the same series of events -- produces better theology, better pastoral care, and closer alignment with what scripture actually says.

**Load-bearing claim:** Premise 1. If someone can demonstrate that operations following different rules are nonetheless manifestations of one deeper operation that itself has explanatory power -- that knowing "it is all one thing" helps you predict what will happen next, or tells you what response is required -- then my argument collapses. But no one has done this. The unity claim adds metaphysical elegance at the cost of practical understanding. And when we are talking about how God reaches suffering human beings, practical understanding is not optional.

Christ's work is not incoherent. It is not random. It flows from one Person, one love, one series of historical events. But it reaches us as distinct operations because we have distinct problems. Calling it one thing does not honor the unity of Christ. It dishonors the specificity of his attention to each distinct dimension of human need.
