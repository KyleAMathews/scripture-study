# The Courtroom Was Never Real: Why Divine Governance Is Parental, Not Juridical

## The Ontological Claim

God is literally the Father of spirits. This is not a metaphor in LDS theology. It is the single most literal statement we can make about God's relationship to human beings. Before there was a plan of salvation, before there was a fall, before there was any law to transgress, there was a Father and there were children. The relationship precedes the legal apparatus entirely. The law was introduced *into* the relationship as a teaching tool; the relationship was not introduced into the law.

When God names Himself -- the moment of ultimate theological self-disclosure in Exodus 34:6 -- He leads with relational language: "The Lord, The Lord God, merciful and gracious, longsuffering, and abundant in goodness and truth." Not "The Lord God, just and impartial, upholding the demands of eternal law." He identifies Himself by His disposition toward His children, not by His fidelity to a code. When the God of all creation tells you who He is, and He describes a parent -- tender, patient, overflowing with goodness -- you should believe Him.

The legal frame enters scripture primarily when *prophets explain God to other people*. Alma uses courtroom language with Corianton. Amulek uses it with the Zoramites. These are pedagogical contexts -- a father trying to get through to a wayward son, a missionary trying to reach a hard audience. The legal metaphor is chosen because it communicates consequence and seriousness in terms mortals can grasp. But when God speaks for Himself -- in Moses 7, in the Prodigal Son, in D&C 19 -- the frame is relational every time. The courtroom is the translation. The household is the original language.

## The Opposing Case, Stated Fairly

The legal-frame argument is not trivial, and I refuse to pretend it is. Its advocates point to the most systematically theological passages in all of LDS scripture and say: look at what these texts actually claim.

Alma 42 states three times that if mercy robbed justice, "God would cease to be God" ([Alma 42:13, 22, 25](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/alma/42.13,22,25)). This appears to be a metaphysical claim, not a pedagogical one -- that justice is constitutive of God's very nature, that it is the foundation His throne rests upon ([Psalms 89:14](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/ps/89.14)), and that without it the entire divine order collapses. D&C 19:15-17 says someone *must* suffer -- either the sinner or Christ -- and "must" is not optional language. The Atonement is described as "satisfying the demands of justice" ([Alma 34:16](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/alma/34.16)), "appeasing" those demands, with Christ standing "betwixt them and justice" ([Mosiah 15:9](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/mosiah/15.9)) as a literal substitute. Justice "claimeth the creature and executeth the law" ([Alma 42:22](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/alma/42.22)) with the autonomy of a self-executing mechanism. In this reading, the Atonement is coherent precisely because it is a real payment to a real demand, and without that transactional structure, Christ's suffering becomes motiveless agony.

This is the strongest version of the case. I understand it. And it is wrong.

## Where the Legal Frame Breaks

The legal frame does not merely strain at the edges. It collapses at precisely the moments when scripture should confirm it most.

**God weeps.** In Moses 7:28-29, the God of heaven looks upon the wicked and weeps. Enoch is so stunned he asks three times how this is possible. And here is the critical structural point: Enoch's confusion *is itself the legal frame failing*. Enoch reasons from the legal frame -- you are just, you are eternal, you are holy, your throne rests on justice; why would you weep? The legal frame predicts a God who enforces without emotion. And what Enoch sees demolishes that prediction. God's answer is entirely parental: "they are the workmanship of mine own hands," "they should choose me, their Father," "they are without affection, and they hate their own blood" ([Moses 7:32-33](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/pgp/moses/7.32-33)). The weeping and the fierce anger coexist in the same being at the same moment (v.28, 34). This is not the profile of a judge. Judges do not weep on the bench. This is the profile of a parent watching a child destroy himself.

**The father runs first.** In Christ's own parable of the Prodigal Son ([Luke 15:20](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/luke/15.20)), the father sees the son "when he was yet a great way off" and runs. The embrace happens *before* the confession. There is no legal proceeding, no satisfaction of demands, no payment tendered, no mediator between the son and justice. The father simply runs. If the legal frame is the actual structure of divine governance, this parable -- told by God Incarnate to illustrate how God actually operates -- is incoherent. The legal-frame reading must treat the Prodigal Son as an incomplete picture, a simplification that leaves out the real mechanism. But Christ did not present it as a simplification. He presented it as the thing itself.

**God says His own legal language is pedagogical scaffolding.** In D&C 19:6-12, God delivers an extraordinary disclosure. He says that "eternal punishment" and "endless punishment" do not mean what they sound like. "Eternal" is His name; the punishment is God's punishment, not punishment that lasts forever. He tells us explicitly: "It is not written that there shall be no end to this torment, but it is written endless torment... that it might work upon the hearts of the children of men" ([D&C 19:6-7](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/dc-testament/dc/19.6-7)). God is *telling us to our faces* that His punitive-sounding language is a pedagogical accommodation designed to produce a specific effect on human hearts. He is pulling back the curtain on His own legal rhetoric and saying: this language serves a purpose, but the purpose is relational -- to work upon your hearts -- not juridical. If God Himself says the legal frame is a teaching tool, on what authority do we insist it is the foundational reality?

**Justice becomes unjust at the edges.** Moroni 8 addresses infant baptism and declares that requiring it would make God "a partial God, and also a changeable God, and a respecter to persons" ([Moroni 8:12](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/moro/8.12)). The logic is devastating: if you apply the legal frame universally -- if all humans are under the demands of justice and require a legal remedy -- then infants, the mentally incapable, and the ignorant are condemned without recourse. Justice *itself* would be unjust. The legal frame, taken as the literal structure of reality, generates a monstrous God. The only escape is to recognize that justice's "demands" are not universal legal claims but contextual descriptions of what happens within a relationship when a knowing agent chooses separation. Where there is no knowing choice, there is no separation, and there is nothing for justice to "demand." The relational frame handles this effortlessly. The legal frame chokes on it.

## What Is at Stake

If you take the legal frame as the actual reality, you get a specific God: a God bound by a law external to Himself, a God whose deepest nature is judicial, a God who *cannot* forgive without first receiving payment. You become a defendant. The Atonement becomes a financial transaction -- a debt paid by a third party so the creditor (justice) releases its claim. Repentance becomes a legal plea. And your relationship to God is fundamentally that of a subject before a court, not a child before a father.

This distortion has consequences. It makes people afraid of God in the wrong way. Not the "fear of the Lord" that is the beginning of wisdom -- the awe of a child before a loving parent of incomprehensible power -- but the fear of a defendant who suspects the judge is looking for reasons to convict. "Come boldly unto the throne of grace" ([Hebrews 4:16](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/heb/4.16)). You do not come boldly before a judge. You come boldly before a parent who loves you. The author of Hebrews knew which frame was real.

It distorts repentance. In the legal frame, repentance is the set of conditions you must meet to activate mercy's claim on your case. In the relational frame, repentance is turning around and discovering that your Father has been running toward you since before you turned. "While we were yet sinners, Christ died for us" ([Romans 5:8](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/rom/5.8)). The mercy preceded the repentance. The love preceded the turning. This is not how courts work. This is how families work.

It distorts the Atonement itself. If Christ's suffering is a payment to satisfy a cosmic creditor, then the Atonement is ultimately about balancing books. But if Christ's suffering is the suffering of a Father and an Elder Brother entering into the full depth of His children's pain -- descending below all things ([D&C 122:8](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/dc-testament/dc/122.8)) so that no child could ever say "You don't understand" -- then the Atonement is about *union*. It is about closing the relational gap. It is about a parent who would rather suffer everything than lose the child.

## The Uncomfortable Conclusion

If the relational frame is the real one, then Alma 42 is Alma doing his best to translate an infinite parental reality into legal terms that his lawyerly son can understand. "God would cease to be God" is true -- but not because an external law would deconstruct Him. It is true because a Father who did not care about His children's choices, who shrugged at their self-destruction, who imposed no consequences and maintained no standards, would not be a Father worth the name. He would "cease to be God" the way a parent who abandons all concern for a child's wellbeing ceases to be a parent. The "demands of justice" are the demands of *love itself* -- love that refuses to pretend sin does not matter, because sin is the thing destroying the child.

"Eternal punishment" is God's punishment -- real, severe, and terrible -- but it is the punishment inherent in separation from a loving Father, not a sentence imposed by a code. The prodigal son suffered on the streets not because a law assigned him suffering but because he had left home. The suffering was real. The father grieved. And the moment the son turned, the father ran.

D&C 19 tells us straight: the punitive language is a pedagogical tool designed to "work upon the hearts of the children of men." The tool is real, the consequences are real, but the *source* of both the consequence and the remedy is a Person, not a principle. A Father, not a framework. The courtroom was always a classroom. The judge was always a parent. The sentence was always a description of what it feels like to leave home.

And the Atonement? The Atonement is a Father who will go to any length -- any suffering, any humiliation, any descent -- to bring His children back. Not to balance a ledger. Not to satisfy an abstraction. To heal a family. To close the distance. To run while the child is yet a great way off.

The courtroom was never real. The family was always the whole story.
