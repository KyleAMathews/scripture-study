# The Power Is in Them: Why "Small and Simple Things" Compound Naturally

## The Ontological Claim

When Jesus described the kingdom of God, he reached for an image so mundane it should have ended the supernatural-multiplication debate before it started. "So is the kingdom of God," he said, "as if a man should cast seed into the ground; and should sleep, and rise night and day, and the seed should spring and grow up, he knoweth not how. For the earth bringeth forth fruit of herself" (Mark 4:26-28). Of herself. Automate, in the Greek -- automatically. The earth produces fruit through its own inherent mechanism, without the farmer understanding or intervening in the process. The farmer sleeps. The seed grows. That is the kingdom.

This is not a throwaway metaphor. It is an ontological claim about how God's purposes unfold, and it is the most neglected parable in Christianity. Jesus is telling us that the mechanism of spiritual growth is built into the fabric of creation itself. The seed does not need a separate miracle for each stage of germination. The DNA is already there. The soil chemistry is already there. The photosynthetic machinery is already there. God's genius is not in reaching down to individually inflate each cell wall -- it is in designing a universe where seeds become trees through processes so elegant they look miraculous from the outside precisely because we cannot see the mechanism.

This is what "small and simple things" actually means. Alma 37:6 -- "by small and simple things are great things brought to pass" -- is not a promise of divine multiplication. It is a description of how compounding works. Small inputs, faithfully repeated, accumulate through behavioral and relational feedback loops until the output dwarfs the input. The missionary in Elder Amos's talk chose two kind acts and two patient acts. He did not receive a supernatural infusion of charity. He triggered a natural upward spiral: his kindness softened his companion, which made it easier to be kind, which deepened the relationship, which transformed the companionship. The mechanism is psychological, relational, organic. The great thing emerged from the small thing the way an oak emerges from an acorn -- not because God reached in to inflate the acorn, but because acorns are *designed to become oaks*.

D&C 58:28 states the principle with a clarity that should make the supernatural-multiplication crowd deeply uncomfortable: "For the power is in them, wherein they are agents unto themselves." Not "the power will be added to them." Not "God will multiply their insufficient power." The power is *in them*. Already. Now. The capacity for transformation is native to human agency. God built compounding into the architecture of the soul the way he built gravity into the architecture of space.

## The Opponent's Strongest Case

The supernatural-multiplication view claims that human effort is categorically insufficient for spiritual transformation. They cite Ether 12:27 -- "I give unto men weakness that they may be humble... then will I make weak things become strong unto them" -- as proof that God must intervene to bridge an unbridgeable gap between human capacity and divine requirement. They point to the loaves and fishes: five loaves cannot feed five thousand through compounding. They invoke Moroni 10:32 -- "his grace is sufficient" -- to argue that grace is something added from outside, a supernatural supplement to natural inadequacy. Their strongest version goes like this: the distance between fallen human nature and Christlike character is infinite, and no amount of compounding can cross an infinite gap. Only grace -- divine power operating outside the natural order -- can make the categorically impossible possible.

## Why It Fails

This argument fails because it misidentifies where God's power operates, and in doing so, it accidentally diminishes the most profound thing God ever did.

Consider what the supernatural-multiplication view actually requires. It requires that God designed a universe where the natural mechanisms he embedded in creation are insufficient for the purposes he created them to serve. It requires that seeds do not really grow into trees -- that at some critical juncture, God must intervene to make the tree happen. It requires that the compounding mechanisms built into human psychology, relationships, habit formation, and neuroplasticity are all ultimately inadequate, and that every genuine spiritual transformation depends on a discrete supernatural intervention.

This is the theological equivalent of what James C. Scott, in *Seeing Like a State*, calls the failure of legibility -- the authoritarian instinct to replace organic, emergent, locally-adapted systems with top-down control because you cannot see the mechanism and therefore assume it does not exist. The supernatural-multiplication view cannot see how kindness compounds into charity, how daily scripture study rewires neural pathways, how repeated prayer reshapes attention and identity -- so it posits an invisible divine hand doing the real work behind the scenes. It makes spiritual growth *legible* by attributing it to a single cause (grace as supernatural intervention) rather than acknowledging the irreducibly complex, organic, emergent process that God actually designed.

Venkatesh Rao nails the pattern: "The big mistake is projecting your subjective lack of comprehension onto the object you are looking at, as 'irrationality.'" The supernatural-multiplication advocate looks at the missionary's transformed companionship, cannot trace the causal chain from two small acts of kindness to a completely different relationship, and concludes that natural compounding was insufficient -- that God must have intervened supernaturally. But the inability to trace a mechanism is not evidence that no mechanism exists. It is evidence that the system is more complex than your model.

## The Deeper Principle

Alma 32 is the most sophisticated treatment of epistemology and spiritual development in all of scripture, and it is relentlessly, exhaustively naturalistic in its imagery. The experiment Alma proposes -- plant the seed, observe whether it swells, whether it enlarges the soul, whether it enlightens the understanding -- is an empirical protocol. The seed "beginneth to be delicious to me." That is not supernatural intervention. That is a feedback signal from a well-designed system. And the instructions for growth are entirely about the practitioner's sustained effort: "nourish the word, yea, nourish the tree as it beginneth to grow, by your faith with great diligence, and with patience" (Alma 32:41). Nourish it. With your diligence. With your patience. The tree grows because you do the work that trees require. By and by -- not through sudden miraculous acceleration -- you pluck the fruit.

James Clear's framework in *Atomic Habits* provides the secular articulation of what Alma described anciently. A 1% daily improvement compounds to a 37x improvement in a year. The mechanism is not mystical. Identity-based habits -- "I am a disciple" rather than "I am trying to be a disciple" -- restructure behavior from the ground up. The four laws (make it obvious, attractive, easy, satisfying) map directly onto the practical structure of Latter-day Saint covenant life: daily scripture study, weekly sacrament, regular temple attendance. These are not offerings laid on an altar waiting for divine multiplication. They are compound interest deposits into an account that grows by the laws God embedded in reality.

What Rao calls "the calculus of grit" illuminates why people give up on small and simple things. They navigate extrinsically -- measuring their progress against external markers of spiritual achievement, looking for the miraculous confirmation that God has multiplied their effort. When it does not come, they conclude the system is broken. But the system was never designed to produce extrinsic confirmation. Spiritual growth, like skill development, "feels effortless from inside, superhuman from outside." The missionary did not experience a dramatic spiritual event. He experienced the gradual, natural, invisible process of becoming kinder. The great thing was the compounding itself, not some separate divine addition.

## The Extreme Version

Here is where I push to the uncomfortable conclusion: grace is not a supernatural supplement to natural process. Grace *is* the natural process. Grace is the name we give to the fact that God designed a universe where seeds become trees, where kindness compounds into charity, where daily practice rewires the brain, where "the earth bringeth forth fruit of herself." The "supernatural" quality of grace is not God breaking the rules of his own creation -- it is God's rules working exactly as designed, producing results that astonish us only because we lack the patience or the perception to watch the compounding happen.

"Without me ye can do nothing," Christ says in the vine metaphor (John 15:5). The supernatural-multiplication reading treats this as evidence that each grape requires a separate miracle. But the metaphor says the opposite. The branch bears fruit because it is connected to the vine. That connection is structural, not episodic. The vine does not intervene grape by grape. The vine provides the constant flow of life -- sap, nutrients, water -- through which branches naturally, organically, automatically produce fruit. Grace is the sap. It flows constantly. It does not need to be specially dispensed for each act of charity, each moment of patience, each flicker of faith. You are connected to the vine, or you are not. If you are connected, the fruit comes. Not because God reaches in to produce it, but because that is what vines do.

Most people's spiritual growth requires zero miraculous intervention. What it requires is sustained connection (covenant life), faithful practice (small and simple things), and patience with the organic timeline of compounding (Alma's "by and by"). The mechanism is in them. The power is in them. God gave them the power when he made them agents unto themselves, embedded compounding into the structure of reality, and said: the seed is good. Plant it. Nourish it. The earth bringeth forth fruit of herself.

## The Inferential Skeleton

My argument is load-bearing at three points:

First, the scriptural metaphors. Every major scriptural treatment of spiritual growth -- Alma 32's seed, Mark 4's seed growing secretly, John 15's vine -- uses organic imagery that describes natural processes. Not a single one of these metaphors depicts God reaching in from outside to override or supplement the growth mechanism. The consistency is overwhelming and, I think, dispositive.

Second, the empirical evidence. The behavioral science of habit formation, compounding, and neuroplasticity provides a complete mechanistic account of how small consistent actions produce dramatic long-term transformation. There is no explanatory gap that requires a supernatural variable. Everything the missionary experienced -- the softening, the reciprocation, the transformation -- is fully explicable through natural psychological and relational dynamics.

Third, the theological logic. A God who must constantly intervene to make his own creation work is a lesser God than one who designed a creation that works. The supernatural-multiplication view, in trying to glorify God's power, actually diminishes his craftsmanship. The deeper glory is not that God can break the rules -- it is that God wrote rules so profound that they produce, through their own natural operation, results that look like miracles. D&C 88:41-43 -- God is in all things, through all things, round about all things. Not intervening from outside, but constitutive of the system itself. The light that "groweth brighter and brighter until the perfect day" (D&C 50:24) is not injected from without. It grows. Brighter. And brighter. By the mechanism God built into light itself.

The power is in them. It always was. That is the most astonishing grace of all.
