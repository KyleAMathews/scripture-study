# The Insufficiency of Everything You Bring

## An Argument That Grace Is the Mechanism, Not the Backdrop

The natural-compounding view of Alma 37:6 goes like this: small faithful actions accumulate through behavioral feedback loops, habit formation, and relational dynamics until they produce great things. The missionary does two kind acts for his companion. The companion softens. Kindness begets kindness. Weeks pass. The companionship transforms. The mechanism is psychological, organic, and entirely natural. God built the capacity for compounding into human nature itself, and "by small and simple things are great things brought to pass" is essentially a description of how habits work. D&C 58:28 confirms it: "the power is in them, wherein they are agents unto themselves."

This view is wrong. Not because it describes nothing real, but because it describes something insufficient and then declares it complete.

---

### I. The Ontological Claim

The mechanism of "small and simple things" is divine multiplication: God taking inadequate human offerings and producing results that exceed the input by orders of magnitude. This is not a poetic way of describing habit formation. It is a claim about what actually happens when a person brings their pitiful two acts of kindness to God in prayer and a broken companionship heals in weeks.

Consider the structure of Ether 12:27 with surgical precision: "I give unto men weakness that they may be humble; and my grace is sufficient for all men that humble themselves before me; for if they humble themselves before me, and have faith in me, then will I make weak things become strong unto them." Three actors, three movements. God gives weakness. The person humbles themselves. God makes weak things strong. The person's contribution is not the raw material of transformation. The person's contribution is the *occasion* for transformation. Humility and faith are the conditions under which God acts, not the mechanism by which strength emerges. The verb is "I will make" — first person, divine agent, active voice. Not "weak things will gradually become strong through practice." *I will make them strong.*

Paul says the same thing from the other direction: "My strength is made perfect in weakness" (2 Corinthians 12:9). The Greek *teleitai* — is perfected, is completed, reaches its telos. Divine strength does not supplement human effort. It reaches its *completion* precisely in the location of human insufficiency. Weakness is not a starting point to be overcome through grit. Weakness is the permanent address where grace operates at full power.

The natural-compounding view cannot account for this. If the mechanism is behavioral feedback loops, then weakness is a problem to be solved, not a condition to be inhabited. Rao's calculus of grit says "hard equals wrong" — if something is ridiculously difficult for you, navigate away from it toward your landscape of strengths. Scripture says the opposite. God *gives* you weakness. He plants you in the soil of your insufficiency on purpose, because that is where grace produces its most characteristic fruit.

---

### II. The Opponent's Strongest Case

The strongest version of the natural-compounding argument is not crude behaviorism. It is the claim that God designed the compounding into reality itself — that grace *is* natural process, seen from a theological altitude. When kindness begets kindness, that is not merely psychology. It is how God structured the moral universe. D&C 50:24, "that which is of God is light; and he that receiveth light, and continueth in God, receiveth more light," describes a system God built. The light compounds because God made it compound. So the distinction between "natural process" and "grace" collapses: grace is the name we give to the divinely authored laws of spiritual growth, just as gravity is the name we give to the divinely authored curvature of spacetime.

This is elegant. It is also fatally incomplete.

---

### III. Why It Fails

It fails because it cannot explain discontinuity.

King Benjamin's people heard a sermon. They did not practice two small acts of charity per day for six months. They did not build relational capital through incremental kindness. They listened, and then: "the Spirit of the Lord Omnipotent has wrought a mighty change in us, or in our hearts, that we have no more disposition to do evil, but to do good continually" (Mosiah 5:2). That is not the output of a behavioral feedback loop. "No more disposition to do evil" is not the far end of a habit-formation curve. It is a phase transition. Something happened to these people that breaks the continuity of natural process.

Alma asks: "Have ye experienced this mighty change in your hearts?" (Alma 5:14). Not "have you gradually improved your behavioral patterns." Not "have you compounded small faithful actions until you reached a tipping point." Have you *experienced* a change — a discrete event, wrought upon you by a power not your own. Mosiah 27:25 specifies what happens: people are "changed from their carnal and fallen state, to a state of righteousness, being redeemed of God, becoming his sons and daughters." And then the consequence: "thus they become new creatures" (Mosiah 27:26). Paul's language in 2 Corinthians 5:17 is identical: "if any man be in Christ, he is a new creature: old things are passed away; behold, all things are become new."

New creature. Not improved creature. Not creature-who-has-accumulated-enough-good-habits. *New.* The language across every dispensation is not the language of gradual accumulation. It is the language of ontological rupture. Something in the person dies, and something that was not there before comes into existence. You cannot get there by compounding. You can only get there by being made.

The natural-compounding view must either deny that these discontinuities are real (reducing them to subjective emotional experiences that the ancient writers described in exaggerated language) or redefine "natural process" so broadly that it includes ontological transformation by an external agent — at which point the word "natural" has lost all meaning and you are just describing grace with unnecessary extra steps.

---

### IV. What the Failure Reveals

The deeper failure of the natural-compounding view is that it is, at root, a theory about skill development applied to the domain of spiritual transformation. And spiritual transformation is not skill development.

Rao's calculus of grit is brilliant at describing how people develop mastery: follow your path of least resistance, build on your strengths, rework and reference your own previous output, release frequently. This is genuinely how competence grows. And it maps beautifully onto the Defer mode of divine engagement — God telling humans to act, to take initiative, to use the capacity already in them.

But the missionary's story is not a story about skill development. The missionary did not become more *skilled* at kindness over six weeks until his competence transformed the companionship. He brought two inadequate acts of kindness to God in prayer, and God did something with them that two acts of kindness cannot do on their own. The loaves and fishes did not compound. Five loaves do not naturally become enough to feed five thousand through some biological feedback loop. A boy offered what he had, and Christ multiplied it beyond any proportion the input could explain.

This is the pattern everywhere in scripture, and the natural-compounding view systematically cannot see it because it has no category for disproportionate output. In a compounding model, output is always a function of input — larger than the initial investment, yes, but mathematically related to it. You can trace the curve. You can show your work. Grace does not show its work. Grace produces results that are *incommensurable* with the offering. The widow's mite does not compound into a fortune. The widow's mite is seen by God, and God provides. The mechanism is not interest rates. The mechanism is a Person.

---

### V. The Deeper Principle

"Without me ye can do nothing" (John 15:5). Not "without me ye can do less" or "without me ye will compound more slowly." Nothing. The branch cannot bear fruit of itself. The vine metaphor is not a description of symbiosis between equal partners. It is a description of radical, ontological dependence. The branch contributes its position on the vine — its abiding, its remaining connected. The vine contributes everything else: the water, the nutrients, the capacity to produce fruit at all. The branch's "small and simple thing" is to stay attached. The fruit is entirely the vine's doing.

This is what D&C 58:28 actually means, and the natural-compounding view reads it exactly backwards. "The power is in them, wherein they are agents unto themselves." Yes — and where did the power come from? Who put it there? The power is *in* them the way sap is *in* the branch: because the vine is pushing it there, continuously, from a source the branch does not control and did not generate. Agency is real. Human action matters. But the power that makes human action fruitful is not native to the human. It is grace, flowing through the connection, producing fruit the branch could never produce alone.

---

### VI. The Uncomfortable Conclusion

Rao's entire framework — the calculus of grit, Mediocratopia, the amble, the path of least resistance — is a sophisticated, secular account of what it feels like to be a branch on a vine when you have forgotten the vine exists. The effortlessness Rao describes ("I went around calling this faux-grit... but the more I looked around me, the more I realized it wasn't hard for them either") is real. But the reason it is effortless is not that these people found their natural strengths. It is that grace is carrying them, and they have mistaken being carried for walking.

This matters because if you think you are walking, you will try to walk faster when results slow down. You will optimize your stride. You will read books about grit and habit formation and compound interest. And when the great things still do not materialize, you will conclude that you are doing it wrong, or that the promise was empty. But you were never walking. You were being carried. And the great things were never yours to produce. They were yours to receive.

The missionary who took two small acts of kindness to God in prayer understood this. He did not say, "I will now practice kindness until it compounds into transformation." He said, "Here is what I have. It is not enough. I am bringing it to You." And God, who has been in the business of making weak things strong since before the foundation of the world, took what was offered and made it into something the missionary could not have made on his own.

That is the mechanism of Alma 37:6. Not compounding. Multiplication. Not natural process. Grace. Not "the power is in them" as a statement about native human capacity, but "the power is in them" as a description of what God continuously pours into every person willing to stay connected to the vine.

By small and simple things are great things brought to pass — not because small things compound, but because God is great, and He has chosen to work through small things, and the distance between the smallness of the offering and the greatness of the result is the exact measure of His grace.
